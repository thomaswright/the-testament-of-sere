# World bible: Sere

Sere is the name used by river pilots for the inhabited basin between the upland chalk shelves, the western salt forests, and the drowned eastern coast. It is neither a planet's name nor a single country. Travelers beyond its watershed report other systems of ancestor law; no narrator has an authoritative view of the whole earth.

## The central fact

Death does not extinguish an undertaking. A promise witnessed by water and a second person survives its speaker, provided somebody continues the prescribed acts of remembrance. The deceased cannot ordinarily speak. They manifest through pressure, condensation, unusual germination, recurrent bodily habits, or the displacement of sounds. Interpretation is a human profession and a source of political power.

An ancestor has only the rights they acquired in life. A king's grave cannot command a spring merely because he was a king. This limitation has generated forgery, strategic marriages, witness kidnapping, and wars over the custody of seemingly insignificant pots. Most supernatural horrors arise when incompatible valid promises are forced onto the same resource. Nobody has proved that the water understands justice.

## Costs and limits

An oath needs an actual witness, contact with actual water, and a repeatable material observance. Its scope must be finite. A vow to protect every future person does nothing. A vow to keep one floodgate open for a named hamlet can bind generations. Dry copies preserve evidence but cannot create an undertaking. Breaking an oath often takes decades to become visible, making causes difficult to establish.

Magic cannot create food, energy, precious metal, or an unlimited labor force. The dead cannot be made into obedient workers. Their manifestations are local, inconsistent, and often destructive. An ancestor's interference can exhaust a spring or salt a field. It cannot power an engine. Changing the terms of an old undertaking requires the consent of surviving claimants, a credible substitute for an extinct claimant, or physical abandonment of the affected place.

## Material life

Work is done by hands, animals, sails, and ordinary mills. Iron is expensive outside forested uplands; copper utensils are repaired for generations. Boats and seasonal pack routes carry bulk goods. Most armies cannot campaign far from harvested grain. A siege threatens an enemy's witnesses and irrigation agreements as much as its walls.

Homes contain practical improvements: glazed drainpipes in prosperous towns, charcoal filters, wind scoops, communal ovens, and ingenious looms. Their existence does not imply factories or universal literacy. Manuscripts, knotted cords, pottery marks, memorized verse, and tally sticks coexist. Everyday knowledge varies by trade and region, rather than advancing along one ladder of civilization.

## Regions

The **Vey floodplain** feeds the lower basin and contains the oldest witness cities. The **Keth chalk shelves** hold cistern settlements and competing burial terraces. The **Orun reedlands** form a river delta whose channels change faster than its legal boundaries. **Talassar** is a western chain of salt marshes, orchards, and boat towns. The **Namar passes** connect upland pasture to southern caravan markets. **Istren** is a rainy northern coast of seasonal fishing villages and timber monasteries. The **Suth gardens** are inland irrigated valleys, famous for grafting and asylum customs. **Avel's islands** lie off the drowned eastern coast and preserve rival versions of the first treaties.

These are geographic groupings, not ethnic essences. Every region contains immigrants, minority practices, political disagreements, and people who do not believe its prevailing account of the dead.

## Narrative discipline

An apparent miracle has a cost and a claimant. A great person depends upon cooks, pilots, scribes, and people who repair things. No species is intrinsically evil. Rival institutions can each preserve something worth saving while producing avoidable harm. A ruin has subsequent occupants, a maintenance history, and a disputed use. Treasure is interesting because someone needs it, can authenticate it, or is endangered by its removal.

The present, 1248, is a period of stressed agreements rather than an apocalypse already decided. Communities can still negotiate, repair, or leave. The central question is how much obligation the living owe to those whose promises they never chose.
