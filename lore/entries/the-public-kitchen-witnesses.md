# The Public Kitchen Witnesses

*Guild · Vey and Orun · Historical coverage: 748–1248 AFR*

Public Kitchen Witnesses authenticate work, provisioning, and dependency through the accounts of communal meals. They do not possess a general power to judge ancestor law. Their evidence is narrower and often more useful: who cooked, who delivered fuel, who ate, whose portion was withheld, and what conditions were attached to food. The guild traces its method to Maren but acknowledges older village kitchens whose records were kept without a formal title. Its founding claim is a lineage of practice, not exclusive invention.

Training begins with purchasing roots for a crowded meal. An apprentice must distinguish weight from edible yield, account for spoiled portions, and record help given by people outside registered households. Only afterward do they learn court forms. This order frustrates patrons who want prestigious scribes rather than experienced cooks. It also makes the witnesses difficult to deceive with a ledger that balances financially while leaving somebody hungry.

The guild has committed its own abuses. During the repair centuries, several kitchens excluded traveling workers to protect their grain reserves while continuing to certify public generosity. An internal inquiry required those kitchens to preserve the names of people refused a meal. The resulting records embarrassed the guild and made its testimony stronger. That experience informs its present rule: a kitchen can refuse a request it cannot meet, but it must not count the refusal as assistance.

The thin springs are testing the rule. More claimants arrive, fuel costs rise, and patrons prefer funding visible ceremonies to buying ordinary millet. Some witnesses propose a collective undertaking guaranteeing a meal across the basin. Others warn that such a promise would exceed the guild's resources and might become a new inherited burden. They are experimenting instead with finite seasonal agreements between named kitchens. The Seed Barges supply several of these routes. In 1248, a network of imperfect local commitments is beginning to replace the grand claim that one beneficent authority feeds everyone. Whether it can survive a bad harvest remains an open and urgent question.
