# The Dispersed Pattern Keepers

*Institution · Keth · Historical coverage: 349–1248 AFR*

Keepers retain local variants and compare failures during annual meetings. A court wants one authorized design. Their counterproposal distinguishes useful common inspection from the erasure of adaptations suited to different water and wool.

The institution’s public account emphasizes its founding purpose, while daily accounts reveal exceptions, unpaid work, and compromises with neighbors. Membership does not imply agreement. A clerk, a patron, and a laborer can defend the same arrangement for incompatible reasons. Its authority is strongest where it provides something people actually need and weakest where it claims that need as proof of permanent jurisdiction. Reform requires deciding who will perform the useful work afterward. Simply abolishing the name may leave the obligation in the hands of less accountable successors.

This belongs to The Thread Houses After the Throne. After the shelf empire fell in 349, displaced weaving households carried patterns, prayers, and water credentials to scattered settlements. A craft organizer tried to coordinate hand spinning through shared inspections and purchasing, claiming inventions actually developed by several workers. A teacher built a network of small hospices and schools whose service mattered more than agreement about his eventual return. Rival courts each recognized the culture as evidence of their own legitimate succession. Weavers used the courts’ competition to preserve local customs, although patrons restricted who could teach. Production remained dispersed, seasonal, and dependent on household labor and finite water access.

In the connected record, [The Unnamed Weavers’ Petition](b016-the-unnamed-weavers-petition.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Local Successor Clause](b016-the-local-successor-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether dispersed schools can choose their own successors without losing supplies pledged through competing courts.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
