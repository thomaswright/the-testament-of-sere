# The Heirs' Drowned Room

*Place · Talassar · Historical coverage: 786–1248 AFR*

The Heirs' Drowned Room lies beneath the oldest counting house in Talassar. It contains transfer records for grain tallies, identity loans, and wartime guarantees. In 786 the room flooded during an unusually high tide. Clerks moved the valuable wooden fragments upstairs but left the accounts documenting how those fragments had changed hands. Later heirs treated the inundation as proof that the records were lost. The water table has fallen since then, and fragments of legible parchment now dry along the upper shelves.

Opening the room presents ordinary physical dangers. The stair has weakened, the air is poor, and salt crystals cause brittle pages to crumble when moved. A conservator can preserve some leaves by supporting them between fine woven screens and drying them slowly. The counting house has repeatedly refused to pay for this work, while paying considerably more to commission portraits of its founders. Its spokesman says uncertain records would encourage fraudulent claims. People with existing claims answer that uncertainty already protects the owners.

The room is also associated with a localized pressure in the drains. Whenever a fragment is carried beyond the threshold without its corresponding account, water rises in the counting house's clean well. The effect ceases when both are returned together. This suggests an undertaking binding ownership to disclosure, but nobody has found its witnessed terms. The heirs can avoid the inconvenience simply by leaving everything in place. Their caution has preserved evidence and obstructed inquiry at the same time.

In 1248 a younger heir has offered Vesh access in exchange for publishing her household's transfers first. He accepts the sequence but refuses any promise that the publication will rehabilitate her ancestors. They have engaged two masons and a screen maker. The project could expose crimes, innocent purchases, and mistakes that nobody intended. Its real obstacle is the expectation that a recovered archive should deliver a single moral verdict, when what the drowned room contains is a long series of transactions whose costs have landed upon different people.
