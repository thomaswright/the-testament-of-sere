# The Succession Without a Throne

*Dispute · Keth · Historical coverage: 349–1248 AFR*

Two courts offer supplies in exchange for naming the teacher’s successor. Houses propose rotating custody and public accounts. Their choice will determine whether useful support strengthens the network or converts service into another disputed imperial inheritance.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The Thread Houses After the Throne. After the shelf empire fell in 349, displaced weaving households carried patterns, prayers, and water credentials to scattered settlements. A craft organizer tried to coordinate hand spinning through shared inspections and purchasing, claiming inventions actually developed by several workers. A teacher built a network of small hospices and schools whose service mattered more than agreement about his eventual return. Rival courts each recognized the culture as evidence of their own legitimate succession. Weavers used the courts’ competition to preserve local customs, although patrons restricted who could teach. Production remained dispersed, seasonal, and dependent on household labor and finite water access.

In the connected record, [Arken of the Shared Spindle](b016-arken-of-the-shared-spindle.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Local Successor Clause](b016-the-local-successor-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether dispersed schools can choose their own successors without losing supplies pledged through competing courts.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
