# The Tuesday Basin Houses

*Institution · Vey · Historical coverage: 208–1248 AFR*

Household meetings let women and servants testify without a temple credential. Their leaders promise freedom from ancestral guilt. Some households treat that promise as permission to retain workshops acquired during confinement, turning a liberating practice into protection for an old advantage.

The institution’s public account emphasizes its founding purpose, while daily accounts reveal exceptions, unpaid work, and compromises with neighbors. Membership does not imply agreement. A clerk, a patron, and a laborer can defend the same arrangement for incompatible reasons. Its authority is strongest where it provides something people actually need and weakest where it claims that need as proof of permanent jurisdiction. Reform requires deciding who will perform the useful work afterward. Simply abolishing the name may leave the obligation in the hands of less accountable successors.

This belongs to The City Beneath the Recitation. Sere’s oldest inland temple was repeatedly rebuilt as river silt raised the surrounding ground. Its custodians recited a single founding poem, although repairs preserved portions of older and contradictory versions. In 903, suspicion of coastal ancestry led Vey’s council to confine thousands of residents in dry compounds. Their former neighbors acquired abandoned workshops. A household prayer movement later promised release from inherited guilt through a simple declaration made beside water. A scholar comparing temple recitations argued that neither the city’s origin nor its wrongdoing could be reduced to one speaker and one cleansing act. His evidence joined the displaced families’ claims to foundations the council called sacred.

In the connected record, [The Dry Compound Removal](b004-the-dry-compound-removal.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Separate Restitution Clause](b004-the-separate-restitution-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether a public act of repentance will restore workshop access or merely authorize the present owners to feel absolved.

Vey households measure wealth partly in dependable access to a public landing. Their markets exchange grain, lamp oil, glazed jars, and legal testimony. A witness can authenticate a transaction but cannot provide the labor needed to fulfill it. Seasonal crews clean the same embankments on which courts announce judgments, and a decision that ignores those crews soon meets an expensive physical limit. Flood deposits preserve small objects while destroying the arrangement that once made their meaning obvious. Recovering an object is therefore easier than establishing what anybody was entitled to do with it.
