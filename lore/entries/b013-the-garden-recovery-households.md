# The Garden Recovery Households

*Institution · Suth · Historical coverage: 1059–1248 AFR*

These households pool seeds after the salt slump but retain separate recipes and planting schedules. Court officials want one uniform assessment. Members explain differences in water and labor, arguing that cooperation depends on recognizing constraints rather than making every plot administratively identical.

The institution’s public account emphasizes its founding purpose, while daily accounts reveal exceptions, unpaid work, and compromises with neighbors. Membership does not imply agreement. A clerk, a patron, and a laborer can defend the same arrangement for incompatible reasons. Its authority is strongest where it provides something people actually need and weakest where it claims that need as proof of permanent jurisdiction. Reform requires deciding who will perform the useful work afterward. Simply abolishing the name may leave the obligation in the hands of less accountable successors.

This belongs to Spring in the Salt House. A small garden town prospered by exporting preserving salts while depending on imported grain. Its merchants claimed descent from a legendary artisan capital, using that story to justify exclusive membership in the exchange court. In 1059 a returning traveler stayed with a married friend whose garden had deteriorated after injury. A quiet play about the household’s competing loyalties became unexpectedly popular. An economic reader used its ordinary transactions to show how trusted institutions reduced bargaining costs for members while burdening outsiders. The salt slump exposed those outsiders’ vulnerability. Excavated workshop remains then complicated the claimed artisan lineage, leaving the town to defend useful cooperation without the exclusive origin story that had supported its authority.

In the connected record, [The Night of the Guest’s Departure](b013-the-night-of-the-guest-s-departure.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Contribution Admission Test](b013-the-contribution-admission-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the exchange court can admit workers lacking prestigious ancestry while protecting the shared guarantees that make ordinary trade possible.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
