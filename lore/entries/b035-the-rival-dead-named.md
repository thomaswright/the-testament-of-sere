# The Rival Dead Named

*Custom · Avel · Historical coverage: 827–1248 AFR*

Spring commemorations name dead from both forces with documented identities. This does not declare their causes equivalent. The practice refuses to make ordinary bereavement depend on accepting the victorious dynasty’s complete interpretation of the battle.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to The Secretary at the Restored Spring. After sixty years sharing a crown with a mainland realm, island nobles restored a separate dynasty in 827. Their secretary, born outside the ruling households, became a capable commander but lacked automatic standing among his officers. Rival exiles attempted a patron-funded landing expecting widespread support and found their boats stranded against defended shallows. Later an advancing mainland force was drawn toward a spring through a controlled withdrawal and halted. Restoration ceremonies joined both episodes into a single inevitable victory, although they involved different loyalties and risks. The secretary’s supply accounts preserve the ordinary decisions beneath legitimacy’s grand language and the uneasy relationship between professed dynastic service and personal survival.

In the connected record, [The Secretary’s Two Allegiances](b035-the-secretary-s-two-allegiances.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Service Before the Lineage Grant](b035-the-service-before-the-lineage-grant.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether restoration grants can be revised to recognize actual service without turning every successful commander’s claim into hereditary authority over the spring.

Avel’s islands preserve competing versions of the basin’s earliest treaties. Sea crossings depend on sails, weather, and provisions, giving a copied text little authority unless somebody can carry its terms home and explain them. Fishing households share beaches with custodians of drowned foundations. Low water reveals masonry that changes both navigation and historical argument. An island’s isolation can shelter dissent and conceal abuse; its effects depend upon who controls the boats. Agreements are usually bounded by named landing places, because a promise to protect an entire sea would have no workable observance.
