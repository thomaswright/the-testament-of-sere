# The Seed Barges

*Fleet · Talassar and Orun · Historical coverage: 612–1248 AFR*

The Seed Barges were seven shallow vessels that met Edrin's departing survivors in the spring of 612. Only four carried seed. The others carried grain, cooking salt, spare rope, and the merchants' household servants. Later paintings omit those three boats because the rescued settlement's founders preferred a story of renewed cultivation to an admission that they still needed somebody else to feed them. The cargo lists survive in fragments held by rival Salt Heirs.

The barges did not return the survivors to their original landing immediately. Their captains required them to camp for nine days while negotiating a safe channel with the neighboring fishers. This delay saved the seed from a late tidal surge but also allowed the merchants to secure transport privileges before the settlers understood the value of the trade they were entering. Benevolence, caution, and advantage traveled together. The captains' descendants emphasize whichever motive best supports their present claims.

No original hull survives. A ceremonial vessel built in 1042 incorporates one old steering pin whose age is uncertain. Every spring it delivers grain to the lower canal kitchens and reed seedlings to households rebuilding a damaged plot. Until recently, recipients had to swear that their aid came from the Salt Heirs alone. Kitchen keepers now record contributions from boat crews, fishers, and donors outside the faction. Several counting houses have withdrawn support rather than accept the longer acknowledgment.

The current flotilla therefore consists of ordinary working boats decorated for a single day's journey. It is smaller than the official festival fleet and reaches more shallow landings. Its organizers are considering an undertaking that would guarantee annual deliveries without assigning permanent control over the recipients' work. The old rescue supplies the reason for trying and the warning against a vague promise. A few households refuse all ceremonial seed and purchase their own. Their independence is expensive, but it ensures that the next hungry generation will not inherit an obligation whose final price nobody can calculate.
