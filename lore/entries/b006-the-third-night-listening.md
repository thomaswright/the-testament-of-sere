# The Third Night Listening

*Custom · Namar · Historical coverage: 667–1248 AFR*

Families sit beside a sleeping relative on the succession anniversary and refrain from answering questions for them. Some transform the custom into divination. Its earliest account is a servant’s protest against ministers treating silence as convenient approval.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to The Feast of the Unawakened Heir. For fifty-three years, King Hevren expanded mountain fortresses using labor bound through inherited household pledges. His numerous descendants occupied competing commands. At a private feast in 667, nine senior relatives died and an injured heir was proclaimed sovereign without awakening. A surviving uncle ordered a winter crossing to secure the southern granaries before rival claimants could respond. Pack herds suffered terribly and the army reached its objective unable to carry food home. A satirist later composed three contradictory succession plays. Court readers preserve only the version flattering their patron; caravan households preserve the versions in which servants, animals, and weather determine the outcome.

In the connected record, [The Three Hevren Plays](b006-the-three-hevren-plays.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Sleeping Hand Limit](b006-the-sleeping-hand-limit.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the crown’s descendants can release coerced labor claims without first agreeing which predecessor possessed the authority to make them.

The Namar passes join pasture settlements to caravan markets. Pack animals carry nearly everything that cannot be made from local stone, wool, or timber. Snow closes crossings irregularly, and a route that is short on a map may cost more food than a longer sheltered path. Hospices preserve undertakings made by travelers who never returned. Their custodians cannot assume that a visitor belongs to one sovereign or one witness tradition. A workable agreement must specify which season, which spring, and which people it protects, rather than claiming a universal welcome the stores cannot support.
