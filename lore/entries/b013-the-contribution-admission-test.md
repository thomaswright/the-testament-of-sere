# The Contribution Admission Test

*Law · Suth · Historical coverage: 1059–1248 AFR*

Applicants can establish court membership through maintained guarantees rather than ancestry alone. Old members demand unusually long probation. The clause’s implementation will determine whether reform actually widens access or merely gives inherited privilege another respectable vocabulary.

The rule’s force depends on a stated scope. It governs named acts and claimants, rather than every future disagreement that resembles its original case. Courts have nevertheless tried to extend it, usually when a narrow precedent would benefit a powerful applicant. Opponents do not have to deny ancestor law to challenge that extension. They can ask which promise was actually made, who witnessed it, and whether the required observance remains possible. The difference between a valid undertaking and a convenient assertion of authority is central to the current dispute.

This belongs to Spring in the Salt House. A small garden town prospered by exporting preserving salts while depending on imported grain. Its merchants claimed descent from a legendary artisan capital, using that story to justify exclusive membership in the exchange court. In 1059 a returning traveler stayed with a married friend whose garden had deteriorated after injury. A quiet play about the household’s competing loyalties became unexpectedly popular. An economic reader used its ordinary transactions to show how trusted institutions reduced bargaining costs for members while burdening outsiders. The salt slump exposed those outsiders’ vulnerability. Excavated workshop remains then complicated the claimed artisan lineage, leaving the town to defend useful cooperation without the exclusive origin story that had supported its authority.

In the connected record, [Femu the Listening Player](b013-femu-the-listening-player.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Contribution Admission Test](b013-the-contribution-admission-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the exchange court can admit workers lacking prestigious ancestry while protecting the shared guarantees that make ordinary trade possible.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
