# The Border’s Missing Review

*Dispute · Avel · Historical coverage: 914–1248 AFR*

Landing workers demand the original review because their work calendars cross the partition. Rival custodians accuse them of abolishing tradition. The workers seek inspection of actual costs first, leaving political arrangements negotiable rather than prejudged by either accusation.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The Houses of Unequal Stars. In 914, island reformers built a community where work rotated according to declared inclination rather than inherited household status. An observer arranged stars by color and apparent brightness, discovering that similar lights could stand at very different distances. Enthusiasts assigned household tasks according to the star table, converting a warning against superficial resemblance into a system of fixed personal destiny. Mainland patrons supplied scribes and introduced a prestigious administrative language distinct from everyday speech. A peace conference divided the islands temporarily between rival custodians. The promised review never occurred, and the community’s experiments became competing traditions on opposite sides of a supposedly provisional line.

In the connected record, [Fen of the Rotating Tables](b007-fen-of-the-rotating-tables.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Seasonal Partition Term](b007-the-seasonal-partition-term.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether a common work calendar can cross the old partition without restoring either the star castes or the patrons’ exclusive scribal authority.

Avel’s islands preserve competing versions of the basin’s earliest treaties. Sea crossings depend on sails, weather, and provisions, giving a copied text little authority unless somebody can carry its terms home and explain them. Fishing households share beaches with custodians of drowned foundations. Low water reveals masonry that changes both navigation and historical argument. An island’s isolation can shelter dissent and conceal abuse; its effects depend upon who controls the boats. Agreements are usually bounded by named landing places, because a promise to protect an entire sea would have no workable observance.
