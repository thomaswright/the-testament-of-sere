# Who Owns the Mill Gesture

*Dispute · Orun · Historical coverage: 537–1248 AFR*

Patrons demand exclusive use of the school’s harvest dances. Mill workers perform the original movements daily. The court must distinguish a commissioned sequence from common bodily knowledge, while teachers seek payment without claiming ownership of work their neighbors cannot stop doing.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The Measure of Living Bread. Orun’s grain towns supplied a distant court through assessed harvest deliveries. In 537, a returning exile founded a public assembly and hired a movement teacher to make its ceremonies legible to crowded quays. The teacher adapted milling, lifting, and kneading gestures into elaborate dances. Workshop experimenters meanwhile discovered that strained bread cultures could continue transforming a mixture after visible yeast sediment was removed. Officials called the process proof that ancestor power inhabited every loaf and tried to tax the cultures. Bakers compared batches and demonstrated that preparation mattered. The assembly’s leader later surrendered local authority to a wider union, leaving schools, granaries, and kitchens to negotiate the consequences of a political unity they had celebrated.

In the connected record, [Dessa of the Mill Dance](b008-dessa-of-the-mill-dance.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Culture Custody Rule](b008-the-culture-custody-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether bread cultures and harvest teaching belong to the households maintaining them or to the authority that sponsors their public performances.

The Orun channels shift across a landscape whose legal descriptions often name vanished banks. Fishers, reed workers, and gardeners share access without sharing a single account of ownership. Transport depends on shallow boats and pilots who read changes in sediment. Salt reaches some plots well before neighboring plots, making an apparently equal assessment unfair in practice. An ancestral claim can survive a change in the channel, but its observance may become impossible at the old site. Communities must decide whether to move the observance, compensate its claimants, or leave the affected water unused.
