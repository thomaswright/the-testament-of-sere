# Culture Keeping

*Trade · Orun · Historical coverage: 537–1248 AFR*

Culture keepers exchange small live portions rather than dry credentials. A starving household may need grain to feed both itself and its starter. Aid agreements account for that practical tension, avoiding promises of endlessly self-sustaining bread.

The trade depends upon tools, training, reliable materials, and people who perform work its public account barely mentions. A skilled craftsperson can improve a process, yet every batch still requires hands and a season of available materials. Output remains bounded by fuel, animals, weather, and the hands available to repair equipment. Prices carry the history of these constraints into households far from the workshop. A commercial undertaking that survives its maker can preserve useful cooperation or prevent successors from adapting to changed conditions. The present negotiation concerns how to retain knowledge while releasing an obligation nobody can now perform.

This belongs to The Measure of Living Bread. Orun’s grain towns supplied a distant court through assessed harvest deliveries. In 537, a returning exile founded a public assembly and hired a movement teacher to make its ceremonies legible to crowded quays. The teacher adapted milling, lifting, and kneading gestures into elaborate dances. Workshop experimenters meanwhile discovered that strained bread cultures could continue transforming a mixture after visible yeast sediment was removed. Officials called the process proof that ancestor power inhabited every loaf and tried to tax the cultures. Bakers compared batches and demonstrated that preparation mattered. The assembly’s leader later surrendered local authority to a wider union, leaving schools, granaries, and kitchens to negotiate the consequences of a political unity they had celebrated.

In the connected record, [Who Owns the Mill Gesture](b008-who-owns-the-mill-gesture.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Culture Custody Rule](b008-the-culture-custody-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether bread cultures and harvest teaching belong to the households maintaining them or to the authority that sponsors their public performances.

The Orun channels shift across a landscape whose legal descriptions often name vanished banks. Fishers, reed workers, and gardeners share access without sharing a single account of ownership. Transport depends on shallow boats and pilots who read changes in sediment. Salt reaches some plots well before neighboring plots, making an apparently equal assessment unfair in practice. An ancestral claim can survive a change in the channel, but its observance may become impossible at the old site. Communities must decide whether to move the observance, compensate its claimants, or leave the affected water unused.
