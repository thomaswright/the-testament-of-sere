# The Door That Opens for No One

*Unresolved place · Keth · Historical coverage: Before 0–1248 AFR*

The name is misleading. The doorway opens easily, and people who cross it usually return without injury. What it does not do is admit a person to the chamber visible beyond it. A traveler steps through beneath a low stone lintel and emerges beside the cistern approach they left, having completed a short circuit nobody can reconcile with the surrounding masonry. Carrying a measured rope produces an ordinary loop. Sending a dry object through without a person leaves it lying on the apparent chamber floor, inaccessible from the returned path.

There are six inspected accounts and many unreliable ones. The inspected accounts agree about the location and duration but not the room’s contents. One traveler saw a broken bench, another a row of empty supports, another shallow water reflecting a ceiling absent from the exterior survey. No account establishes treasure, a sleeping god, or a universal route into death. Visitors seeking those things have supplied the more spectacular stories.

The doorway stands above the drowned chamber reached by Liwen’s bronze bell. His measurements establish old masonry beneath it without showing that the two spaces share a functioning entrance. Patrons who claim an ancient capital here are relying on resemblance and political hope, not a confirmed plan of the ruin. The Inspection Is Not Title Rule applies with particular force: reaching an extraordinary threshold does not purchase ownership of the people maintaining its approach.

Its only repeatable material cost is water lost from a nearby plaster cistern after a crossing. The amount varies with the visitor’s wet footwear, not rank or declared purpose. Custodians limit trials and require provision for that loss. They forbid leaving people inside deliberately, a prohibition adopted after a frightened apprentice was told that obedience would prove his household’s innocence. The apprentice returned through the ordinary loop, but the attempted coercion remains recorded.

One lintel mark may indicate a household undertaking whose intended claimant is extinct. If that reading is correct, the apparent room might be available only to somebody the living cannot honestly appoint. If it is wrong, generations of legal interpretation have been imposed on an older phenomenon. Custodians preserve both possibilities. They maintain the roof, admit careful inquiry, and refuse the profitable promise that a sufficiently worthy adventurer will be allowed through.
