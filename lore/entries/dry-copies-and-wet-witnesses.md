# Dry Copies and Wet Witnesses

*Foundational distinction · Vey · Historical coverage: 0–1248 AFR*

A dry copy can establish what somebody said, where an object traveled, or which portion remains unpaid. It cannot create the undertaking it describes. A clerk may copy a promise perfectly and still lack the speaker’s consent, the actual water contact, or the credible second witness required for the promise to survive death. This distinction makes ordinary archives useful without turning every copied mistake into a supernatural burden.

Water contact alone is equally insufficient. A child splashing beside a game has not agreed to every rule written by a patron elsewhere. A person singing a wordless air cannot endorse a charter whose terms were never stated. The living often try to expand consent through these shortcuts because direct negotiation is slower, less flattering, and capable of producing refusal. The basin’s courts differ in how well they resist that pressure.

The wet witness need not remain alive forever. Evidence can pass through custody, independent accounts, material observance, and testimony about what the witness actually heard. None supplies unlimited certainty. A genuine bowl authenticates its own history only within what surviving evidence establishes; it does not make every prestigious story attached to it true. The Heirs’ Drowned Room is dangerous precisely because a valid object and a missing account can preserve incompatible halves of a claim.

Dispersed catalog houses carry dry references between regions, allowing a local inquiry to find evidence far away. This has widened access and created new risks when a reference exposes a vulnerable household’s location. The Withheld Location Clause permits private inspection through a trusted custodian, preserving accountability without assuming that every useful fact must be shouted in a crowded yard.

The First Reckoning’s achievement was to record obligations beyond the life of a ruling seal. Its continuing danger is the temptation to treat the record as the obligation itself. In 1248, careful readers still begin with three separate questions: what happened, what was agreed, and what can now be performed. An elegant manuscript can assist each inquiry. It cannot answer all three merely by being old, beautifully made, or guarded by an influential house.
