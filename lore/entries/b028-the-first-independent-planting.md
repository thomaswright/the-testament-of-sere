# The First Independent Planting

*Event · Vey · Historical coverage: 721–1248 AFR*

New plot holders planted before shared gate schedules were revised. Some crops flourished and others dried. The uneven result documents practical constraints, not proof that independence was universally foolish or that every communal tradition deserved restoration unchanged.

The event survives through records made for different purposes. A household remembers hunger; a court records jurisdiction; a custodian records the interruption of an observance. Their dates can be reconciled without making their explanations identical. Later commemorations often select a decisive moment because a ceremony needs a beginning and an end. The consequences were less orderly, traveling through marriages, prices, and people who moved away. An honest account follows those consequences far enough to distinguish a temporary outcome from an agreement that still constrains the present.

This belongs to The Four Rooms of the River Crown. Four command stations once regulated the river crossing, but three withdrew and one endured through successive cultures. Their foundations preserved different measures of tenancy. In 721 an ailing ruler delegated practical decisions to an unobtrusive heir, who invited schools and craft collections into the river capital. A minister offered gardeners individual plots outside communal tenure while enforcing harsh emergency courts against revolt. His killing interrupted the reform before its water consequences were resolved. New owners inherited land but not necessarily sufficient channel access. The river crown’s four old rooms now house collections of soil, tools, names, and seed, preserving the landscape’s layered history while households seek terms capable of making their plots productive.

In the connected record, [The Regency’s Plain Key Ring](b028-the-regency-s-plain-key-ring.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Soil and Channel Attachment](b028-the-soil-and-channel-attachment.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether private plot titles can be reconciled with communal channel obligations without forcing households back into arrangements they legitimately hoped to leave.

Vey households measure wealth partly in dependable access to a public landing. Their markets exchange grain, lamp oil, glazed jars, and legal testimony. A witness can authenticate a transaction but cannot provide the labor needed to fulfill it. Seasonal crews clean the same embankments on which courts announce judgments, and a decision that ignores those crews soon meets an expensive physical limit. Flood deposits preserve small objects while destroying the arrangement that once made their meaning obvious. Recovering an object is therefore easier than establishing what anybody was entitled to do with it.
