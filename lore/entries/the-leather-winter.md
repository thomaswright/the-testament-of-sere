# The Leather Winter

*Event · Orun · Historical coverage: 611–612 AFR*

The Leather Winter is named for the final entries in the provisioning book of Edrin Settlement: harness leather, boiled shoe uppers, and strips cut from the witness tent. Later chronicles make these meals proof of heroic endurance. The provisioning book describes something less flattering. The settlers had brought ornamental trees, several crates of burial tiles, and almost no equipment for fishing a tidal river. Their sponsors expected to find valuable stone under the marsh. They found mud, brackish water, and neighbors unwilling to surrender existing fishing grounds.

Hunger did not affect every household equally. The overseer's family retained a private grain chest until its locks were broken in the fourth month. Unregistered servants received portions only after registered settlers had eaten. One servant, whose name survives only as an altered ration mark, moved the cooking shelter to a neighbor's bank and exchanged repaired nets for fish. This arrangement fed forty people while official negotiations remained stalled over the neighbors' refusal to acknowledge the settlers' land grant.

By spring 612, the survivors had agreed to leave. Talassari barges met them at the channel mouth with seed, grain, and a proposal for fragrant reed cultivation. The return was celebrated as a second founding. It also began the Green Tithe, whose original ambiguity would encumber descendants for centuries. The rescuer's kindness and the rescuer's advantage cannot be separated into different episodes; they arrived in the same boats.

Present observances consist of repairing a stranger's fishing net and cooking one meal without grain. Wealthier houses instead display ornamental shoes carved from expensive wood. Local historians object that these objects repeat the founders' priorities with remarkable accuracy. The surviving portion of the witness tent is kept in a dry storehouse, where it smells of smoke whenever a ration dispute reaches the river court. Officials sometimes call this a warning from the first settlers. Their descendants ask whether the warning is against hunger, vanity, or the habit of converting another person's rescue into an inheritable claim.
