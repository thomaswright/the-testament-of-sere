# The Late Measure Choir

*Institution · Suth · Historical coverage: 982–1248 AFR*

The choir admits singers unable to climb the terraces and assigns runners to carry their verses. This division preserves elderly testimony. Younger organizers want every member visibly present at festivals, a requirement that would exclude the people who remember the earliest sequence.

The institution’s public account emphasizes its founding purpose, while daily accounts reveal exceptions, unpaid work, and compromises with neighbors. Membership does not imply agreement. A clerk, a patron, and a laborer can defend the same arrangement for incompatible reasons. Its authority is strongest where it provides something people actually need and weakest where it claims that need as proof of permanent jurisdiction. Reform requires deciding who will perform the useful work afterward. Simply abolishing the name may leave the obligation in the hands of less accountable successors.

This belongs to The Steps That Carry a Song. Suth’s burial terraces expanded for centuries, accommodating rulers, gardeners, pilgrims, and people denied formal household graves. In 982 a valley campaign classified several gardening communities as supporters of an armed revolt and destroyed their water records. Survivors carried names through sung measures adapted from an older harvesting repertoire. Runners relayed the verses across terraces, sometimes abandoning a timed contest to support an exhausted companion. Later courts trusted official burial inscriptions more than the songs. Comparing the two revealed that prestigious monuments had reused stone from destroyed communal channels. The present dispute concerns testimony embedded in performance and material, neither of which supplies an easy complete account.

In the connected record, [The Winter of Unmarked Burials](b005-the-winter-of-unmarked-burials.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Testimony in Measure Rule](b005-the-testimony-in-measure-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: who must maintain the terraces while descendants seek acknowledgment of the gardens their monuments displaced.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
