# The Quiet Guest Meal

*Custom · Suth · Historical coverage: 1059–1248 AFR*

Guests receive a task before being asked to explain their intentions. The custom began as household tact, allowing useful presence without immediate confession. Some hosts exploit it as unpaid labor, so current versions limit both the task and the stay.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to Spring in the Salt House. A small garden town prospered by exporting preserving salts while depending on imported grain. Its merchants claimed descent from a legendary artisan capital, using that story to justify exclusive membership in the exchange court. In 1059 a returning traveler stayed with a married friend whose garden had deteriorated after injury. A quiet play about the household’s competing loyalties became unexpectedly popular. An economic reader used its ordinary transactions to show how trusted institutions reduced bargaining costs for members while burdening outsiders. The salt slump exposed those outsiders’ vulnerability. Excavated workshop remains then complicated the claimed artisan lineage, leaving the town to defend useful cooperation without the exclusive origin story that had supported its authority.

In the connected record, [Spring Behind a Damaged Wall](b013-spring-behind-a-damaged-wall.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Contribution Admission Test](b013-the-contribution-admission-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the exchange court can admit workers lacking prestigious ancestry while protecting the shared guarantees that make ordinary trade possible.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
