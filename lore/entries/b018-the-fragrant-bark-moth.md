# The Fragrant Bark Moth

*Creature · Talassar · Historical coverage: 1206–1248 AFR*

Moths breed beneath bark left to recover after harvest. Buyers demand complete stripping, reducing later yield. Producers preserve seasonal limits through local agreements whose practical reasoning the merchants’ fantastic origin tales make difficult to hear.

Reports describe a living creature before they describe a supernatural emblem. It needs food, shelter, and a place to breed, and it reacts to disturbances that witnesses may misunderstand. A keeper who recognizes those needs can often prevent the encounter later remembered as a curse. The animal cannot be made into an obedient instrument of ancestral power. Where an undertaking changes its habitat, the effect is indirect and costly to other inhabitants. Stories that treat its behavior as a moral verdict obscure both ordinary suffering and the human decisions that altered the site.

This belongs to The Tour of the Fragrant Ruins. In 1206, a retired basin governor toured southern landing towns to encourage reopening fragrant-bark trade. He held no formal office but his presence changed appointments and credit. A painter accompanying him produced grand images of restored prosperity, while a nearly motionless singer performed laments for workers lost on earlier voyages. Merchants circulated fantastic stories about the bark’s remote source to protect routes and prices. The tour helped revive genuine employment but also bypassed local review of old concessions. Present households compare painted abundance, songs of vulnerability, and actual cargo accounts to decide which promises were made and which audiences merely assumed from a powerful visitor’s approval.

In the connected record, [The Governor’s Southern Circuit](b018-the-governor-s-southern-circuit.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Signature Before the Praise](b018-the-signature-before-the-praise.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether renewed trade can preserve local oversight when the commitments were encouraged by an influential person who signed no accountable undertaking.

Talassar combines salt marshes, sheltered boat towns, and orchards whose irrigation depends on tidal timing. Merchants trade durable claims alongside perishable cargo, although only an actual witnessed undertaking has force after death. A repaired boat, a borrowed name, and a neglected drain can each determine whether a contract remains practical. Houses that prosper from distant obligations are exposed to local maintenance costs they would prefer to call somebody else’s problem. Seasonal kitchens and landing crews keep accounts that often describe the region more accurately than its ceremonial histories of uninterrupted family success.
