# The Sour Mark Board

*Artifact · Orun · Historical coverage: 873–1248 AFR*

The board’s layers show warnings corrected and reused. A commemorative painter removed the corrections to make the crisis look orderly. Restoring them recovers the practice that distinguished useful caution from a confident but unreliable rumor.

Material inspection places limits on the object’s legend. Repairs, wear, and changes in custody can establish that it was used repeatedly without proving every story attached to it. Its value is relational: somebody can authenticate it, somebody needs it for an observance, and somebody may suffer if it is removed. An expensive replacement would not automatically inherit those relations. Custodians therefore record loans and repairs as carefully as ceremonial appearances. A collector who purchases the object but ignores its attached undertaking may own something that cannot safely be taken home.

This belongs to The Diary of the Fog Muster. A provincial commander pursued raiders into a narrow lakeside road during morning mist, convinced that delay would damage his reputation. His force was trapped between water and hidden slopes. Survivors returned to towns where a separate levy attempt had triggered running confrontations along several roads. A household diarist recorded provisions, embarrassment, conflicting rumors, and the gap between expected courage and actual fear. A comic performer later adapted the diary’s domestic quarrels. Authorities preferred a grand account of one awakening people, but the small records reveal uneven preparation and changing choices. They also preserve meals, messages, and repairs that enabled resistance more reliably than the flattering language of destiny.

In the connected record, [The Meal Before the Muster](b029-the-meal-before-the-muster.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Levy’s Stated Purpose](b029-the-levy-s-stated-purpose.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how commemorations can honor real resistance without using its heroic image to impose another compulsory levy on the households that sustained it.

The Orun channels shift across a landscape whose legal descriptions often name vanished banks. Fishers, reed workers, and gardeners share access without sharing a single account of ownership. Transport depends on shallow boats and pilots who read changes in sediment. Salt reaches some plots well before neighboring plots, making an apparently equal assessment unfair in practice. An ancestral claim can survive a change in the channel, but its observance may become impossible at the old site. Communities must decide whether to move the observance, compensate its claimants, or leave the affected water unused.
