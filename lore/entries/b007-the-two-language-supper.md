# The Two Language Supper

*Custom · Avel · Historical coverage: 914–1248 AFR*

Hosts recite each undertaking in the patron’s language and a household’s chosen tongue. Mistakes are corrected before food is served. Younger speakers propose a third version for migrants, extending a custom whose original defenders once treated two languages as sufficient forever.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to The Houses of Unequal Stars. In 914, island reformers built a community where work rotated according to declared inclination rather than inherited household status. An observer arranged stars by color and apparent brightness, discovering that similar lights could stand at very different distances. Enthusiasts assigned household tasks according to the star table, converting a warning against superficial resemblance into a system of fixed personal destiny. Mainland patrons supplied scribes and introduced a prestigious administrative language distinct from everyday speech. A peace conference divided the islands temporarily between rival custodians. The promised review never occurred, and the community’s experiments became competing traditions on opposite sides of a supposedly provisional line.

In the connected record, [The Conference Afterword](b007-the-conference-afterword.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Seasonal Partition Term](b007-the-seasonal-partition-term.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether a common work calendar can cross the old partition without restoring either the star castes or the patrons’ exclusive scribal authority.

Avel’s islands preserve competing versions of the basin’s earliest treaties. Sea crossings depend on sails, weather, and provisions, giving a copied text little authority unless somebody can carry its terms home and explain them. Fishing households share beaches with custodians of drowned foundations. Low water reveals masonry that changes both navigation and historical argument. An island’s isolation can shelter dissent and conceal abuse; its effects depend upon who controls the boats. Agreements are usually bounded by named landing places, because a promise to protect an entire sea would have no workable observance.
