# The Executioner's Jars

*Phenomenon · Vey · Historical coverage: 777–1248 AFR*

Three storage jars belonged to the household of the man who executed Maren's assassin. Their surfaces bear ordinary marks for oil, grain, and washing water. Witnesses in 777 claimed that the jars repeated Maren's pouring song despite being empty. The earliest testimony came from the executioner's niece, who described the sound as the rhythm of ladling rather than recognizable words. A later clerk embellished it into a complete lament and supplied verses absent from the household account.

The council ordered the jars buried separately in dry ground. Two were recovered when the executioner's descendants moved house. The third remained beneath a courtyard and was broken by a drain repair in 946. Its fragments entered the fill used to raise a nearby lane. People walking that lane during rain sometimes report an additional footstep, but this claim appeared in writing only after the fragments' origin became public. The evidence supports caution more readily than certainty.

The intact jars passed between households unwilling to destroy them and unable to sell them honestly. Their current custodian keeps one filled with clean water and the other with grain reserved for the public kitchen. Under this arrangement, the reported sound has ceased. Advocates of the Daughters' Petition interpret the silence as acceptance of the sentence the council refused to impose: continuing provision for the interrupted gatherings. Potters suggest that changing temperature and load altered the jars' acoustics. The custodian sees no need to choose, since feeding the kitchen is useful under either explanation.

Traveling collectors have offered enough money to repair her whole roof. She refuses because removing the jars would end the arrangement before anyone knows whether it has finally discharged the old obligation. Her neighbors disagree about the wisdom of this refusal. Some contribute roofing straw; others call her frightened and wasteful. The jars therefore sustain a present argument about cost, obligation, and evidence. Their smallness is essential to that argument. A monstrous apparition would settle nothing about who ought to buy next month's grain.
