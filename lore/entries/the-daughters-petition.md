# The Daughters' Petition

*Text · Vey · Historical coverage: 777–1248 AFR*

The Daughters' Petition survives in three copies, none written by the daughters themselves. Ena dictated the household account to a market scribe. Her sister Sul corrected it orally before the court. The official clerk incorporated those corrections but omitted the sentence explaining why the sisters feared an execution beside the river. A kitchen apprentice copied the omitted sentence onto the reverse of a flour receipt. The flour receipt is now the most contested legal text in Vey.

The petition asked that their mother's murderer be kept alive, working under supervision to supply food for the gatherings he had interrupted. This was not an appeal to forgiveness without consequence. Ena proposed a permanent obligation measured in sacks of grain. Sul proposed that the murderer be forbidden to witness any undertaking, denying him the social standing through which he had approached Maren. Their recommendations would have made him dependent upon people he had tried to silence.

Council advocates later praised the sisters as gentle mourners and quoted the petition at festivals. Their quotations stopped before the proposed sentence. Present opponents make the opposite mistake, treating the whole document as a technical warning about water while ignoring the sisters' refusal to manufacture a second bereaved household. The kitchen copy holds both arguments together. Its final line asks who would feed the assassin's elderly aunt after his death.

In 1248, prisoners' relatives cite the petition to demand that courts describe the practical consequences of a punishment before imposing it. Magistrates accept the principle when it concerns bridge labor and reject it when it concerns their own fees. A traveling copy has reached the reed districts, where cutters use its accounting method to calculate the costs of hereditary contracts. The text's authority is therefore widening beyond its original dispute. There is no proof that either daughter intended this legacy. Their surviving grocery accounts suggest they were chiefly concerned with keeping the public kitchen open through the winter after their mother died.
