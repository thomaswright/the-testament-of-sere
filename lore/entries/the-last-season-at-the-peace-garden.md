# The Last Season at the Peace Garden

*Current agreement · Suth and Orun · Historical coverage: 1247–1248 AFR*

The Peace Garden’s oldest food grant belongs to a hosting household with no presently recognized recipient. Its custodian died in 1247, leaving grain assigned to delegates who now arrive under four rival seals. A victorious downstream patron has offered to assume the grant if the garden admits only his approved negotiators. Gardeners who maintain the place consider that a purchase of neutrality’s appearance, not a continuation of the original hospitality.

A walking judge proposed Last Season instead. The gardeners would appoint a temporary recipient for one harvest, document actual meals, and review the arrangement before renewal. The substitute would acquire no title to the garden and no general right to choose delegates. Before appointment, catalog carriers are searching for unrecognized descendants and migrants who might hold a genuine part of the old claim. Absence in the local register is not being treated as convenient proof that the household is extinct.

The immediate obstacle is transport. Grain boats must use a ferry jurisdiction whose operators favor registered ancestral households. Alternative-crossing organizers from Orun have offered pilots and copies of the Paid Passage Equality rule. Their support is bounded: they can supply three inspected voyages, not an endless route financed by admiration for peace. Garden custodians will repair a landing in return and record who benefits through the Repair Assessment practice developed among Namar’s rival capital offices.

Care houses in the reedlands need some of the same grain. Their keepers propose an Emergency Portion Substitution that would reduce delegate rations modestly while guaranteeing staple meals in the wards. Some diplomats call this an insult to negotiation. The gardens’ cooks answer with actual quantities, showing how much grain unusually large entourages consume.

The agreement remains unsigned in 1248. Small suppliers have been invited to inspect it, because reducing their portions to fund a generous public settlement would simply move hunger out of view. The garden’s name still promises freedom from armed occupation, but its present hope is more demanding: a finite network of meals, crossings, repairs, and review that lets talks continue without one patron acquiring the households whose labor keeps the apparently neutral place alive.
