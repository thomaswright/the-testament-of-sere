# The Plateau of Open Mouths

*Battlefield and pilgrimage · Namar · Historical coverage: 472–1248 AFR*

The mouths are neither human remains nor statues. They are low clay vents set above old underground storage pits on the plateau. During the coalition battle of 472, cooks used the pits to keep grain cool and wounded people sheltered from sun. Later victory accounts describe them as the places where fallen ancestors swallowed an invading king's breath. The cooks' surviving tallies record beans, smoke, and inadequate bedding. They also record a sound that nobody present could place: one long exhalation from a pit already emptied.

After the armies departed, the local shrine claimed the sound as an oracle's confirmation that the coalition had saved the basin. Families whose dead came from different sides were offered commemorative places only if they accepted that wording. Several declined. They built low stone shelters around the pits where their relatives had actually been tended, marking names without endorsing a shared explanation for the battle. Some shelters remain; others supplied stone for the winter hospice when its first roof failed.

Travelers still encounter the exhalation at particular vents. It extinguishes a small flame held close to the opening and leaves frost on a wet cord even during warm weather. Removing the cord ends that visible effect. Air keepers have compared empty pits, shaded pits, and pits with different amounts of moisture. The accounts support a local disturbance, not a voice that can pronounce upon the legitimacy of every ruler passing through. No traveler has reliably extracted a word from it. Several profitable guides have claimed otherwise.

The most serious cost is exhaustion. A person sleeping beside an active vent can wake unable to complete an ordinary breath without turning toward the opening. Hospice workers move sufferers to the sheltered western path and take turns keeping them awake until the habit loosens. Recovery can take days. Nobody requires a confession from the afflicted. The plateau's reminders are dangerous regardless of whether a visitor's ancestors fought there, and efforts to treat ancestry as a medical explanation have delayed useful care.

In 1248 pilgrims tie strips of plain cloth to three poles above the old supply camp. Each strip represents a journey completed home, rather than an enemy defeated. Shrine officials tolerate the practice because travelers buy meals nearby, though they dislike its refusal to produce a victorious lineage. On quiet evenings, the poles knock against one another while the vents below remain still. When an exhalation does come, the cloth does not move. The hospice door opens, a lantern is shaded, and somebody carries blankets uphill.
