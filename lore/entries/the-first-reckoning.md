# The First Reckoning

*Foundational event · Vey · Historical coverage: 0–89 AFR*

The First Reckoning did not count the dead. It counted promises whose speakers had died, a distinction preserved in the oldest inscription beneath Vey’s public landing. Before the Reckoning, each ruling household dated obligations by the reign that authenticated them. When a ruler fell, successors announced a new beginning. The water did not reliably cooperate. Gates resisted opening, fields salted unevenly, and households discovered that changing a seal could not discharge a predecessor’s witnessed commitment.

The immediate crisis concerned a ferry whose grain portion had been pledged twice. One dead keeper had promised a fixed amount to workers repairing its landing; his successor had promised the same stored grain to people sheltering flood survivors. Both agreements were credible. The landing households tried washing the successor’s mark from the boat. That act changed nothing except the appearance of the hull. They eventually divided available grain, negotiated remaining portions with surviving claimants, and repaired a second landing so the ferry could earn the balance.

Readers from six cities adopted a common count of years to prevent future rulers from hiding obligations inside incompatible reign calendars. They did not elect a basin sovereign. Each city retained its courts and rites, and several refused the count for generations. Year zero therefore marks an agreement about records, not creation, universal revelation, or the first appearance of the dead. Traditions describing it as the morning water learned human names are beautiful and historically unproved.

The first lists also recorded uncertainty. A damaged tally was not repaired by inventing a comforting obligation. An unnamed claimant was not assumed to be nobody. These practices influenced the later Faceted Water Council, whose founders expanded coordination but repeatedly mistook it for general authority.

In 1248, the Reckoning still helps people compare accounts across fallen regimes. Its original ferry has vanished; its landing has been rebuilt at least nine times. A small shelf beneath the present kitchen holds an ordinary bowl for obligations still under inquiry. The bowl has no known supernatural effect. Its significance is the pause it creates before an official declares that a change of ruler has solved somebody else’s unfinished life.
