# The Common Spring Exception

*Law · Namar · Historical coverage: 731–1248 AFR*

Travelers may drink without declaring a provincial name, but cannot establish a tenancy through that act alone. Courts repeatedly confuse drinking with settlement. The exception’s custodians carry separate cups and tenancy registers to make the boundary visible in practice.

The rule’s force depends on a stated scope. It governs named acts and claimants, rather than every future disagreement that resembles its original case. Courts have nevertheless tried to extend it, usually when a narrow precedent would benefit a powerful applicant. Opponents do not have to deny ancestor law to challenge that extension. They can ask which promise was actually made, who witnessed it, and whether the required observance remains possible. The difference between a valid undertaking and a convenient assertion of authority is central to the current dispute.

This belongs to The Provinces of the Held Breath. The pass province was renamed New Ascents after annexation in 731, although its inhabitants continued calling it Ulven. A family wrestling school taught caravan laborers to escape restraint without drawing weapons, frustrating patrols who relied upon public submission. A dramatist adapted these lessons into a puppet story about frightened carriers transporting a volatile funerary resin. Provincial officers condemned the play while privately hiring its author to entertain their households. Independence assemblies used the school’s breathing exercises to organize quiet meetings. A later division of the province placed relatives under different courts and turned a shared technique into competing claims of national inheritance.

In the connected record, [Savel the Condemned Player](b003-savel-the-condemned-player.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Common Spring Exception](b003-the-common-spring-exception.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how travelers can use the shared springs without being forced to endorse either provincial history.

The Namar passes join pasture settlements to caravan markets. Pack animals carry nearly everything that cannot be made from local stone, wool, or timber. Snow closes crossings irregularly, and a route that is short on a map may cost more food than a longer sheltered path. Hospices preserve undertakings made by travelers who never returned. Their custodians cannot assume that a visitor belongs to one sovereign or one witness tradition. A workable agreement must specify which season, which spring, and which people it protects, rather than claiming a universal welcome the stores cannot support.
