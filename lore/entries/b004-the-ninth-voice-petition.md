# The Ninth Voice Petition

*Dispute · Vey · Historical coverage: 208–1248 AFR*

A variant recitation grants arrivals access to kilns for one firing season. Descendants invoke it against exclusive clay rights. The court must decide whether restoring the old obligation requires a present cooperative schedule or the impossible reconstruction of an extinct household.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The City Beneath the Recitation. Sere’s oldest inland temple was repeatedly rebuilt as river silt raised the surrounding ground. Its custodians recited a single founding poem, although repairs preserved portions of older and contradictory versions. In 903, suspicion of coastal ancestry led Vey’s council to confine thousands of residents in dry compounds. Their former neighbors acquired abandoned workshops. A household prayer movement later promised release from inherited guilt through a simple declaration made beside water. A scholar comparing temple recitations argued that neither the city’s origin nor its wrongdoing could be reduced to one speaker and one cleansing act. His evidence joined the displaced families’ claims to foundations the council called sacred.

In the connected record, [Orvel the Unenrolled Reader](b004-orvel-the-unenrolled-reader.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Separate Restitution Clause](b004-the-separate-restitution-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether a public act of repentance will restore workshop access or merely authorize the present owners to feel absolved.

Vey households measure wealth partly in dependable access to a public landing. Their markets exchange grain, lamp oil, glazed jars, and legal testimony. A witness can authenticate a transaction but cannot provide the labor needed to fulfill it. Seasonal crews clean the same embankments on which courts announce judgments, and a decision that ignores those crews soon meets an expensive physical limit. Flood deposits preserve small objects while destroying the arrangement that once made their meaning obvious. Recovering an object is therefore easier than establishing what anybody was entitled to do with it.
