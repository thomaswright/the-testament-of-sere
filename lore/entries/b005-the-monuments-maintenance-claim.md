# The Monuments’ Maintenance Claim

*Dispute · Suth · Historical coverage: 982–1248 AFR*

Royal custodians ask displaced descendants to keep repairing walls above their former gardens. Descendants offer finite paid work while demanding accurate inscriptions. The disagreement turns on whether necessary maintenance can be separated from recognition of the structures’ contested origins.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The Steps That Carry a Song. Suth’s burial terraces expanded for centuries, accommodating rulers, gardeners, pilgrims, and people denied formal household graves. In 982 a valley campaign classified several gardening communities as supporters of an armed revolt and destroyed their water records. Survivors carried names through sung measures adapted from an older harvesting repertoire. Runners relayed the verses across terraces, sometimes abandoning a timed contest to support an exhausted companion. Later courts trusted official burial inscriptions more than the songs. Comparing the two revealed that prestigious monuments had reused stone from destroyed communal channels. The present dispute concerns testimony embedded in performance and material, neither of which supplies an easy complete account.

In the connected record, [Lethra of the Late Measure](b005-lethra-of-the-late-measure.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Testimony in Measure Rule](b005-the-testimony-in-measure-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: who must maintain the terraces while descendants seek acknowledgment of the gardens their monuments displaced.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
