# The Salt Garden Burrowing Dove

*Creature · Suth · Historical coverage: 1059–1248 AFR*

Ground-nesting doves favor abandoned salt pits with scattered shrubs. Reopening pits destroys nests, while unchecked scrub damages channels. Gardeners plan alternating use, resisting the convenient choice between treating every abandoned place as sacred and reopening everything for income.

Reports describe a living creature before they describe a supernatural emblem. It needs food, shelter, and a place to breed, and it reacts to disturbances that witnesses may misunderstand. A keeper who recognizes those needs can often prevent the encounter later remembered as a curse. The animal cannot be made into an obedient instrument of ancestral power. Where an undertaking changes its habitat, the effect is indirect and costly to other inhabitants. Stories that treat its behavior as a moral verdict obscure both ordinary suffering and the human decisions that altered the site.

This belongs to Spring in the Salt House. A small garden town prospered by exporting preserving salts while depending on imported grain. Its merchants claimed descent from a legendary artisan capital, using that story to justify exclusive membership in the exchange court. In 1059 a returning traveler stayed with a married friend whose garden had deteriorated after injury. A quiet play about the household’s competing loyalties became unexpectedly popular. An economic reader used its ordinary transactions to show how trusted institutions reduced bargaining costs for members while burdening outsiders. The salt slump exposed those outsiders’ vulnerability. Excavated workshop remains then complicated the claimed artisan lineage, leaving the town to defend useful cooperation without the exclusive origin story that had supported its authority.

In the connected record, [The Outsiders’ Exchange Walk](b013-the-outsiders-exchange-walk.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Contribution Admission Test](b013-the-contribution-admission-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the exchange court can admit workers lacking prestigious ancestry while protecting the shared guarantees that make ordinary trade possible.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
