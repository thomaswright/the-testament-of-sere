# The Refuge Membership Hearing

*Dispute · Avel · Historical coverage: 809–1248 AFR*

Earlier residents seek council seats and access to protected landings. Passenger heirs fear losing guarantees against reenslavement. A revised charter can preserve those finite protections while changing representation, provided negotiators stop treating equality as cancellation of rescue.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The Rock of Borrowed Refuge. The hollow rock guarding Avel’s southern strait accumulated shrines, walls, and competing harbor names over centuries. In 809 a privateer captain transported people freed from distant labor claims to a settlement beside it. The settlers proclaimed independence but restricted full membership to households arriving through the captain’s patrons. Existing island residents supplied food and knowledge while remaining outside the new council. A performer played two opposed brothers in a harbor drama, giving their rival fears equal weight. The captain’s apprentice mapped shelters omitted from official charts. His work revealed both genuine rescue and new hierarchy, leaving present residents to negotiate freedom without erasing the people already living beneath the celebrated rock.

In the connected record, [Taven of the Two Brothers](b017-taven-of-the-two-brothers.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Equal Landing Witness](b017-the-equal-landing-witness.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether founding passengers and earlier island households can share harbor authority without losing the protective landing agreements that enabled refuge.

Avel’s islands preserve competing versions of the basin’s earliest treaties. Sea crossings depend on sails, weather, and provisions, giving a copied text little authority unless somebody can carry its terms home and explain them. Fishing households share beaches with custodians of drowned foundations. Low water reveals masonry that changes both navigation and historical argument. An island’s isolation can shelter dissent and conceal abuse; its effects depend upon who controls the boats. Agreements are usually bounded by named landing places, because a promise to protect an entire sea would have no workable observance.
