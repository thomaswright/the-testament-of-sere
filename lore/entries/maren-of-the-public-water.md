# Maren of the Public Water

*Person · Vey · Historical coverage: 723–776 AFR*

Maren was a cook before she was a witness, and remained a cook after acquiring the title. Her authority came from a small undertaking made in 748: she promised to provide one hot meal to any person who had spent a day assisting with the river's public maintenance. The promise attracted laborers whose names did not appear in the city's household lists. When disagreements arose about their work, Maren's kitchen accounts supplied evidence that official registers lacked. She became a witness because people already relied upon her records.

Her early decisions were practical rather than visionary. She counted a borrowed tool as a contribution only if its owner helped repair it. She accepted children's work when the work was safe and refused adults who assigned dangerous tasks to children. Her most unpopular ruling required masters to include servants in the distribution of meals earned by a household. The river court upheld it narrowly, recognizing a cook's right to decide whom she fed without recognizing the servants as independent claimants.

During the negotiations of 776, Maren insisted that public kitchen water join the water presented by rival authorities. She understood that most inhabitants would experience the agreement through bread prices, tolls, and access to canal labor, not through the titles its delegates bestowed upon themselves. The assassin accused her of making the city's ancestral rights available to outsiders. That accusation was false in law: kitchen testimony could establish labor, not transfer an ancestor's land. It was effective politically because it made ordinary recognition sound like dispossession.

Her descendants reject the smooth-faced statues sold beside the Bridge of Mercies. A surviving list shows that she was often impatient, occasionally miscounted, and once refused to hear a petition until its speaker helped peel roots. Those faults make her procedures more intelligible than sainthood does. Her unfinished meal promise remains attached to the Third Bowl, but her descendants believe it can eventually be discharged by completing the maintenance work for which the murdered day's meal had been owed. Nobody has yet established which stretch of bank those workers repaired.
