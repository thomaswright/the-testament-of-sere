# The Eighteen Accounts Compared

*Text · Talassar · Historical coverage: 758–1248 AFR*

The comparison reveals multiple houses charging for the same ridge repair. Some charges bought materials that never arrived. Its compiler distinguishes duplicated accounting from deliberate theft, permitting precise repayment claims instead of one satisfying accusation against the whole confederation.

Copies disagree in ways that matter. A shortened phrase can remove a condition, and a copied date can imply that a later amendment belonged to the original undertaking. Dry writing preserves evidence but cannot make an oath binding by itself. Readers must distinguish a record of consent from a writer’s assertion that consent occurred. Marginal notes are sometimes the only surviving testimony of people excluded from the formal version. Establishing a trustworthy reading therefore depends on comparing custody, material, and ordinary expenses, as well as elegant arguments about wording.

This belongs to The Lacquered Beasts of the Portage. After a river dynasty collapsed in 758, rival commanders divided its landings among eighteen allied households. Their agreements failed to account for the portage connecting sheltered waters across a rain-soaked ridge. A sword assessor established a craft settlement there, bringing lacquer workers, gardeners, and puppet builders together. One builder made moving beast models whose incremental gestures demonstrated how loaded animals struggled on the track. Commanders commissioned grand performances of their victories while supplying too little food to real carriers. A southern advance stopped within sight of its destination because its crews and animals could carry no more. The craft records preserve practical limits obscured by the confederation’s succession chronicles.

In the connected record, [The Lacquered Footprints](b010-the-lacquered-footprints.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Burden Inspection Right](b010-the-burden-inspection-right.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether portage households can set carrying limits against rulers who inherited toll rights but no credible maintenance duties.

Talassar combines salt marshes, sheltered boat towns, and orchards whose irrigation depends on tidal timing. Merchants trade durable claims alongside perishable cargo, although only an actual witnessed undertaking has force after death. A repaired boat, a borrowed name, and a neglected drain can each determine whether a contract remains practical. Houses that prosper from distant obligations are exposed to local maintenance costs they would prefer to call somebody else’s problem. Seasonal kitchens and landing crews keep accounts that often describe the region more accurately than its ceremonial histories of uninterrupted family success.
