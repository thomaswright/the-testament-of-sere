# Funerary Resin Carrying

*Trade · Namar · Historical coverage: 731–1248 AFR*

Resin carriers use small separated jars, trading efficiency for reduced fire risk. Buyers insist on larger loads and describe caution as cowardice. The school helps workers negotiate maximum burdens, making a martial reputation useful without requiring another spectacular confrontation.

The trade depends upon tools, training, reliable materials, and people who perform work its public account barely mentions. A skilled craftsperson can improve a process, yet every batch still requires hands and a season of available materials. Output remains bounded by fuel, animals, weather, and the hands available to repair equipment. Prices carry the history of these constraints into households far from the workshop. A commercial undertaking that survives its maker can preserve useful cooperation or prevent successors from adapting to changed conditions. The present negotiation concerns how to retain knowledge while releasing an obligation nobody can now perform.

This belongs to The Provinces of the Held Breath. The pass province was renamed New Ascents after annexation in 731, although its inhabitants continued calling it Ulven. A family wrestling school taught caravan laborers to escape restraint without drawing weapons, frustrating patrols who relied upon public submission. A dramatist adapted these lessons into a puppet story about frightened carriers transporting a volatile funerary resin. Provincial officers condemned the play while privately hiring its author to entertain their households. Independence assemblies used the school’s breathing exercises to organize quiet meetings. A later division of the province placed relatives under different courts and turned a shared technique into competing claims of national inheritance.

In the connected record, [Who May Name Ulven](b003-who-may-name-ulven.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Common Spring Exception](b003-the-common-spring-exception.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how travelers can use the shared springs without being forced to endorse either provincial history.

The Namar passes join pasture settlements to caravan markets. Pack animals carry nearly everything that cannot be made from local stone, wool, or timber. Snow closes crossings irregularly, and a route that is short on a map may cost more food than a longer sheltered path. Hospices preserve undertakings made by travelers who never returned. Their custodians cannot assume that a visitor belongs to one sovereign or one witness tradition. A workable agreement must specify which season, which spring, and which people it protects, rather than claiming a universal welcome the stores cannot support.
