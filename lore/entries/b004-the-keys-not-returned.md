# The Keys Not Returned

*Text · Vey · Historical coverage: 208–1248 AFR*

This booklet pairs welcome speeches with unresolved workshop claims. Its compiler avoids saying every present owner is guilty, which disappoints some campaigners. Precise ownership histories nevertheless make it harder for officials to dismiss the whole collection as indiscriminate accusation.

Copies disagree in ways that matter. A shortened phrase can remove a condition, and a copied date can imply that a later amendment belonged to the original undertaking. Dry writing preserves evidence but cannot make an oath binding by itself. Readers must distinguish a record of consent from a writer’s assertion that consent occurred. Marginal notes are sometimes the only surviving testimony of people excluded from the formal version. Establishing a trustworthy reading therefore depends on comparing custody, material, and ordinary expenses, as well as elegant arguments about wording.

This belongs to The City Beneath the Recitation. Sere’s oldest inland temple was repeatedly rebuilt as river silt raised the surrounding ground. Its custodians recited a single founding poem, although repairs preserved portions of older and contradictory versions. In 903, suspicion of coastal ancestry led Vey’s council to confine thousands of residents in dry compounds. Their former neighbors acquired abandoned workshops. A household prayer movement later promised release from inherited guilt through a simple declaration made beside water. A scholar comparing temple recitations argued that neither the city’s origin nor its wrongdoing could be reduced to one speaker and one cleansing act. His evidence joined the displaced families’ claims to foundations the council called sacred.

In the connected record, [The Basin That Will Not Clear](b004-the-basin-that-will-not-clear.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Separate Restitution Clause](b004-the-separate-restitution-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether a public act of repentance will restore workshop access or merely authorize the present owners to feel absolved.

Vey households measure wealth partly in dependable access to a public landing. Their markets exchange grain, lamp oil, glazed jars, and legal testimony. A witness can authenticate a transaction but cannot provide the labor needed to fulfill it. Seasonal crews clean the same embankments on which courts announce judgments, and a decision that ignores those crews soon meets an expensive physical limit. Flood deposits preserve small objects while destroying the arrangement that once made their meaning obvious. Recovering an object is therefore easier than establishing what anybody was entitled to do with it.
