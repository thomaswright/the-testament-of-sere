# Hand Spindle Coordination

*Trade · Keth · Historical coverage: 349–1248 AFR*

Shared purchasing reduces waste but cannot eliminate seasonal labor limits. Workers accept bounded delivery agreements. Patrons seeking endless output must provide wool, food, and time rather than invoke the organizer’s legendary ingenuity.

The trade depends upon tools, training, reliable materials, and people who perform work its public account barely mentions. A skilled craftsperson can improve a process, yet every batch still requires hands and a season of available materials. Output remains bounded by fuel, animals, weather, and the hands available to repair equipment. Prices carry the history of these constraints into households far from the workshop. A commercial undertaking that survives its maker can preserve useful cooperation or prevent successors from adapting to changed conditions. The present negotiation concerns how to retain knowledge while releasing an obligation nobody can now perform.

This belongs to The Thread Houses After the Throne. After the shelf empire fell in 349, displaced weaving households carried patterns, prayers, and water credentials to scattered settlements. A craft organizer tried to coordinate hand spinning through shared inspections and purchasing, claiming inventions actually developed by several workers. A teacher built a network of small hospices and schools whose service mattered more than agreement about his eventual return. Rival courts each recognized the culture as evidence of their own legitimate succession. Weavers used the courts’ competition to preserve local customs, although patrons restricted who could teach. Production remained dispersed, seasonal, and dependent on household labor and finite water access.

In the connected record, [The Succession Without a Throne](b016-the-succession-without-a-throne.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Local Successor Clause](b016-the-local-successor-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether dispersed schools can choose their own successors without losing supplies pledged through competing courts.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
