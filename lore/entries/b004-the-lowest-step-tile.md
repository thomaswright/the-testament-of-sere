# The Lowest Step Tile

*Artifact · Vey · Historical coverage: 208–1248 AFR*

A loosened tile records a meal obligation in an archaic script. Temple officials claim it belongs to an unrelated building. Its mortar matches the earliest stair, while its food quantities align with old landing accounts, making dismissal increasingly difficult.

Material inspection places limits on the object’s legend. Repairs, wear, and changes in custody can establish that it was used repeatedly without proving every story attached to it. Its value is relational: somebody can authenticate it, somebody needs it for an observance, and somebody may suffer if it is removed. An expensive replacement would not automatically inherit those relations. Custodians therefore record loans and repairs as carefully as ceremonial appearances. A collector who purchases the object but ignores its attached undertaking may own something that cannot safely be taken home.

This belongs to The City Beneath the Recitation. Sere’s oldest inland temple was repeatedly rebuilt as river silt raised the surrounding ground. Its custodians recited a single founding poem, although repairs preserved portions of older and contradictory versions. In 903, suspicion of coastal ancestry led Vey’s council to confine thousands of residents in dry compounds. Their former neighbors acquired abandoned workshops. A household prayer movement later promised release from inherited guilt through a simple declaration made beside water. A scholar comparing temple recitations argued that neither the city’s origin nor its wrongdoing could be reduced to one speaker and one cleansing act. His evidence joined the displaced families’ claims to foundations the council called sacred.

In the connected record, [The Declaration of Clear Hands](b004-the-declaration-of-clear-hands.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Separate Restitution Clause](b004-the-separate-restitution-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether a public act of repentance will restore workshop access or merely authorize the present owners to feel absolved.

Vey households measure wealth partly in dependable access to a public landing. Their markets exchange grain, lamp oil, glazed jars, and legal testimony. A witness can authenticate a transaction but cannot provide the labor needed to fulfill it. Seasonal crews clean the same embankments on which courts announce judgments, and a decision that ignores those crews soon meets an expensive physical limit. Flood deposits preserve small objects while destroying the arrangement that once made their meaning obvious. Recovering an object is therefore easier than establishing what anybody was entitled to do with it.
