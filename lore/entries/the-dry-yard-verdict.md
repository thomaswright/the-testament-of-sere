# The Dry Yard Verdict

*Legal precedent · Vey · Historical coverage: 777–1248 AFR*

The Dry Yard Verdict holds that an execution conducted beyond the reach of a river undertaking cannot automatically transfer the condemned person's unfinished obligations into that river. The principle arose when Vey's council rejected the Daughters' Petition but accepted its warning about the location of punishment. Officials moved Maren's assassin to a paved yard with no visible water. They covered the executioner's drinking cup and required witnesses to arrive without wet footwear. The care given to these precautions is documented more fully than the council's consideration of the proposed alternative sentence.

Later jurists found the verdict narrower than the officials intended. A yard can contain buried drains, damp earth, and water carried inside living bodies. The precedent never proved that these forms counted as contact, but it also failed to establish that visible dryness was sufficient. Courts gradually replaced its physical test with an accounting test: had the execution created a new claimant whose dependency was foreseeable? This revision made the assassin's elderly aunt more relevant than the paving stones.

Punishment did not disappear. Some towns used the new test to require provision for a prisoner's dependents. Others charged the provision back to those dependents as a hereditary debt. The same protective language could therefore soften a sentence or extend its reach. Kitchen witnesses began recording the actual flow of food and money rather than accepting a court's statement that provision had been made. Their accounts remain the best evidence for distinguishing these outcomes.

In 1248, a reed district wants to invoke the verdict against a counting house that destroyed a debtor's home during repossession. The house argues that repossession is not punishment. The petitioners argue that the legal label matters less than the predictable creation of hunger. Their case depends upon whether a precedent about death can govern deliberate exposure to winter. It will be heard near the Bridge of Mercies, where the original council's tidy distinction between technical safety and human consequence has never entirely survived contact with daily life.
