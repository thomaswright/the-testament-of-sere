# The First Interrupted Chorus

*Event · Keth · Historical coverage: 1162–1248 AFR*

Officers stopped a performance when current prices entered the refrain. The audience completed the quantities from memory. Officials then permitted older accounts, demonstrating that their objection concerned present accountability more than the supposed impropriety of singing debt.

The event survives through records made for different purposes. A household remembers hunger; a court records jurisdiction; a custodian records the interruption of an observance. Their dates can be reconciled without making their explanations identical. Later commemorations often select a decisive moment because a ceremony needs a beginning and an end. The consequences were less orderly, traveling through marriages, prices, and people who moved away. An honest account follows those consequences far enough to distinguish a temporary outcome from an agreement that still constrains the present.

This belongs to The Song of the Measured Depths. Keth’s deep cistern diggers bought food from the same houses that held their labor debts. In 1162 a composer and a low-voiced singer built an all-night drama from their accounts, connecting household voices across generations. Council opponents resigned offices expecting the remaining authority to compromise; a disciplined faction filled the vacancies and gained uncontested custody of the witness basins. Independent measurers found that several disputed basins changed depth in distinct steps rather than continuously as additional promises were presented. The finding challenged established interpretations but did not enable unlimited power or labor. Performers used the measured intervals to organize a drama whose public clarity made both coercion and political assumptions harder to conceal.

In the connected record, [The Digger’s Split Meal Bowl](b019-the-digger-s-split-meal-bowl.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Finite Depth Offering](b019-the-finite-depth-offering.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether displaced diggers can renegotiate store debts before the new basin custodians classify every refusal as a failure of sacred duty.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
