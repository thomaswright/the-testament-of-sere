# The Revived Garden Readers

*Institution · Suth · Historical coverage: 631–1248 AFR*

Readers teach suppressed household speech using several scripts. Patrons prefer one purified form. The group records regional differences and migrant additions, preserving intelligibility without pretending revival requires returning to a uniform language that never existed.

The institution’s public account emphasizes its founding purpose, while daily accounts reveal exceptions, unpaid work, and compromises with neighbors. Membership does not imply agreement. A clerk, a patron, and a laborer can defend the same arrangement for incompatible reasons. Its authority is strongest where it provides something people actually need and weakest where it claims that need as proof of permanent jurisdiction. Reform requires deciding who will perform the useful work afterward. Simply abolishing the name may leave the obligation in the hands of less accountable successors.

This belongs to The Women Who Named the Hidden Country. Garden migrants carried a coastal place name inland until it became the name of several distinct settlements. Later patrons suppressed their administrative institutions but could not erase household speech. In 631 a materials negotiator organized scarce timber and metal during war, then sought agreements with former enemies. He was murdered amid accusations about his outsider ancestry. Women gathering in garden parlors wrote elaborate wonder tales whose hidden countries unsettled the courts’ official map of belonging. A language revival collected their work beside older oral accounts, sometimes mistaking polished literary invention for unchanged folklore. Present readers preserve both forms and question which histories were chosen to make a people appear uniform after migration.

In the connected record, [The Negotiator’s Road Murder](b031-the-negotiator-s-road-murder.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Speech Without Descent Test](b031-the-speech-without-descent-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether revived language institutions can welcome their actual mixed descendants without replacing old exclusion with a newly polished ancestry test.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
