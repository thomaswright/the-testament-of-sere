# The Old Debt Price Review

*Dispute · Talassar · Historical coverage: 1123–1248 AFR*

Workers seek payments measured against current food costs. Creditors invoke authentic old undertakings. The hearing considers finite substitutions and compensated releases, requiring more practical work than declaring either inheritance sacred or every old promise meaningless.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The Empty Cloth Negotiations. After several seasons of falling prices in 1123, Talassar merchants reduced wages while their stored obligations retained old values. A mural painter simplified laboring figures into bright curved forms visible across crowded quays. A public reader investigated a councilor’s splendid house and found that its builder also received favorable canal contracts. The reader lost her sponsored platform but continued recitations in market yards. Negotiators began carrying plain cloth to request safe passage, although some courts treated the sign as surrender and others as a request to speak. Confusion allowed creditors to seize people approaching in good faith. Shared standards emerged slowly from recorded incidents rather than a single heroic proclamation.

In the connected record, [Leven of the Curved Workers](b012-leven-of-the-curved-workers.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Approach Without Submission](b012-the-approach-without-submission.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how negotiated access and old debts can be revised while households still need the contracts maintaining their canals.

Talassar combines salt marshes, sheltered boat towns, and orchards whose irrigation depends on tidal timing. Merchants trade durable claims alongside perishable cargo, although only an actual witnessed undertaking has force after death. A repaired boat, a borrowed name, and a neglected drain can each determine whether a contract remains practical. Houses that prosper from distant obligations are exposed to local maintenance costs they would prefer to call somebody else’s problem. Seasonal kitchens and landing crews keep accounts that often describe the region more accurately than its ceremonial histories of uninterrupted family success.
