# Names at the Meal Table

*Custom · Namar · Historical coverage: 731–1248 AFR*

Hosts ask guests what they call the province only after serving food. This lets travelers answer without immediate hunger hanging upon the choice. Some wealthy households now perform the courtesy while withholding lodging, narrowing the protection it originally provided.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to The Provinces of the Held Breath. The pass province was renamed New Ascents after annexation in 731, although its inhabitants continued calling it Ulven. A family wrestling school taught caravan laborers to escape restraint without drawing weapons, frustrating patrols who relied upon public submission. A dramatist adapted these lessons into a puppet story about frightened carriers transporting a volatile funerary resin. Provincial officers condemned the play while privately hiring its author to entertain their households. Independence assemblies used the school’s breathing exercises to organize quiet meetings. A later division of the province placed relatives under different courts and turned a shared technique into competing claims of national inheritance.

In the connected record, [The Unnamed Province Atlas](b003-the-unnamed-province-atlas.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Common Spring Exception](b003-the-common-spring-exception.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how travelers can use the shared springs without being forced to endorse either provincial history.

The Namar passes join pasture settlements to caravan markets. Pack animals carry nearly everything that cannot be made from local stone, wool, or timber. Snow closes crossings irregularly, and a route that is short on a map may cost more food than a longer sheltered path. Hospices preserve undertakings made by travelers who never returned. Their custodians cannot assume that a visitor belongs to one sovereign or one witness tradition. A workable agreement must specify which season, which spring, and which people it protects, rather than claiming a universal welcome the stores cannot support.
