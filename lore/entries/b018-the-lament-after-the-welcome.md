# The Lament After the Welcome

*Custom · Talassar · Historical coverage: 1206–1248 AFR*

Hosts invite a voyage lament after praising restored commerce. Merchants call the song discouraging. Laborers say remembering losses is part of negotiating acceptable risk, making welcome and warning compatible rather than competing ceremonial moods.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to The Tour of the Fragrant Ruins. In 1206, a retired basin governor toured southern landing towns to encourage reopening fragrant-bark trade. He held no formal office but his presence changed appointments and credit. A painter accompanying him produced grand images of restored prosperity, while a nearly motionless singer performed laments for workers lost on earlier voyages. Merchants circulated fantastic stories about the bark’s remote source to protect routes and prices. The tour helped revive genuine employment but also bypassed local review of old concessions. Present households compare painted abundance, songs of vulnerability, and actual cargo accounts to decide which promises were made and which audiences merely assumed from a powerful visitor’s approval.

In the connected record, [The Bark’s Impossible Homeland](b018-the-bark-s-impossible-homeland.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Signature Before the Praise](b018-the-signature-before-the-praise.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether renewed trade can preserve local oversight when the commitments were encouraged by an influential person who signed no accountable undertaking.

Talassar combines salt marshes, sheltered boat towns, and orchards whose irrigation depends on tidal timing. Merchants trade durable claims alongside perishable cargo, although only an actual witnessed undertaking has force after death. A repaired boat, a borrowed name, and a neglected drain can each determine whether a contract remains practical. Houses that prosper from distant obligations are exposed to local maintenance costs they would prefer to call somebody else’s problem. Seasonal kitchens and landing crews keep accounts that often describe the region more accurately than its ceremonial histories of uninterrupted family success.
