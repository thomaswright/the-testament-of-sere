# The Sleeping Hand Limit

*Law · Namar · Historical coverage: 667–1248 AFR*

A hand touching water cannot authenticate an undertaking unless a witness establishes conscious assent. The ruling threatens old royal contracts. Creditors seek exceptions for sovereigns, claiming ordinary limits would make succession too precarious to finance.

The rule’s force depends on a stated scope. It governs named acts and claimants, rather than every future disagreement that resembles its original case. Courts have nevertheless tried to extend it, usually when a narrow precedent would benefit a powerful applicant. Opponents do not have to deny ancestor law to challenge that extension. They can ask which promise was actually made, who witnessed it, and whether the required observance remains possible. The difference between a valid undertaking and a convenient assertion of authority is central to the current dispute.

This belongs to The Feast of the Unawakened Heir. For fifty-three years, King Hevren expanded mountain fortresses using labor bound through inherited household pledges. His numerous descendants occupied competing commands. At a private feast in 667, nine senior relatives died and an injured heir was proclaimed sovereign without awakening. A surviving uncle ordered a winter crossing to secure the southern granaries before rival claimants could respond. Pack herds suffered terribly and the army reached its objective unable to carry food home. A satirist later composed three contradictory succession plays. Court readers preserve only the version flattering their patron; caravan households preserve the versions in which servants, animals, and weather determine the outcome.

In the connected record, [Taren of the Servants’ Door](b006-taren-of-the-servants-door.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Sleeping Hand Limit](b006-the-sleeping-hand-limit.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the crown’s descendants can release coerced labor claims without first agreeing which predecessor possessed the authority to make them.

The Namar passes join pasture settlements to caravan markets. Pack animals carry nearly everything that cannot be made from local stone, wool, or timber. Snow closes crossings irregularly, and a route that is short on a map may cost more food than a longer sheltered path. Hospices preserve undertakings made by travelers who never returned. Their custodians cannot assume that a visitor belongs to one sovereign or one witness tradition. A workable agreement must specify which season, which spring, and which people it protects, rather than claiming a universal welcome the stores cannot support.
