# The Garden Record Burnings

*Event · Suth · Historical coverage: 982–1248 AFR*

Soldiers burned channel rolls while calling them rebel lists. Some officers preserved copies to assess taxes afterward. Those administrative duplicates now undermine the official claim that destroyed gardens had been abandoned, revealing continuity through records never intended as memorials.

The event survives through records made for different purposes. A household remembers hunger; a court records jurisdiction; a custodian records the interruption of an observance. Their dates can be reconciled without making their explanations identical. Later commemorations often select a decisive moment because a ceremony needs a beginning and an end. The consequences were less orderly, traveling through marriages, prices, and people who moved away. An honest account follows those consequences far enough to distinguish a temporary outcome from an agreement that still constrains the present.

This belongs to The Steps That Carry a Song. Suth’s burial terraces expanded for centuries, accommodating rulers, gardeners, pilgrims, and people denied formal household graves. In 982 a valley campaign classified several gardening communities as supporters of an armed revolt and destroyed their water records. Survivors carried names through sung measures adapted from an older harvesting repertoire. Runners relayed the verses across terraces, sometimes abandoning a timed contest to support an exhausted companion. Later courts trusted official burial inscriptions more than the songs. Comparing the two revealed that prestigious monuments had reused stone from destroyed communal channels. The present dispute concerns testimony embedded in performance and material, neither of which supplies an easy complete account.

In the connected record, [The Rootstock Plaque](b005-the-rootstock-plaque.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Testimony in Measure Rule](b005-the-testimony-in-measure-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: who must maintain the terraces while descendants seek acknowledgment of the gardens their monuments displaced.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
