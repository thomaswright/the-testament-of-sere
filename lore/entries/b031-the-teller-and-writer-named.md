# The Teller and Writer Named

*Custom · Suth · Historical coverage: 631–1248 AFR*

Readings identify both known oral tellers and literary authors. Unknown origins remain unknown. The custom counters the assumption that giving credit to one contributor must erase everyone else or that anonymity makes a story morally available for any appropriation.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to The Women Who Named the Hidden Country. Garden migrants carried a coastal place name inland until it became the name of several distinct settlements. Later patrons suppressed their administrative institutions but could not erase household speech. In 631 a materials negotiator organized scarce timber and metal during war, then sought agreements with former enemies. He was murdered amid accusations about his outsider ancestry. Women gathering in garden parlors wrote elaborate wonder tales whose hidden countries unsettled the courts’ official map of belonging. A language revival collected their work beside older oral accounts, sometimes mistaking polished literary invention for unchanged folklore. Present readers preserve both forms and question which histories were chosen to make a people appear uniform after migration.

In the connected record, [The Names Before Migration](b031-the-names-before-migration.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Speech Without Descent Test](b031-the-speech-without-descent-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether revived language institutions can welcome their actual mixed descendants without replacing old exclusion with a newly polished ancestry test.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
