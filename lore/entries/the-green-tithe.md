# The Green Tithe

*Institution · Orun · Historical coverage: 612–1248 AFR*

The Green Tithe began as a rescue loan. In 612, Talassari merchants supplied starving settlers with millet, fishing line, and seed for a fragrant reed used to protect stored cloth from moths. The settlers promised one tenth of the reed harvest until the merchants' descendants had recovered the expense. They did not specify a value for the loan, and the merchants did not disclose how rapidly the reed would sell in the upland markets. The undertaking was witnessed beside a ditch that later became the district's main irrigation channel.

Within three generations, reed cultivation displaced much of the settlement's food production. Buyers offered handsome prices to households with spare labor, then used debts to acquire the harvests of households without it. The original tithe expanded through separate contracts into compulsory cutting days, canal maintenance, and a claim upon a child's apprenticeship. These were human agreements authenticated by human courts. The old ancestors sustained only the original tenth, although monopoly advocates repeatedly represented every later exaction as sacred.

The Heret rebels proved the difference in 782 by destroying a warehouse while leaving the tithe's tenth untouched. No ancestral manifestation followed. The monopoly's guards nevertheless punished them as oathbreakers, and the courts declined to investigate during the campaign. This distinction—between a binding undertaking and the violence justified in its name—remains central to the Green Tithe's history.

In 1248, the monopoly sells excellent moth reed and maintains several canals that genuinely prevent flooding. Those services do not cancel its inherited claims. A reform council proposes paying the ancient tenth directly into a common granary, recognizing the original merchants' heirs as recipients rather than owners of the workers. The Salt Heirs oppose the change because it would expose how little of their authority has supernatural force. Reed cutters oppose parts of it too: a grain assessment would punish households whose marsh plots are too saline to grow grain. A workable settlement will require new irrigation and new accounting, not merely the destruction of a villain's ledger.
