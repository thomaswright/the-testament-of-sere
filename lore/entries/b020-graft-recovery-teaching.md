# Graft Recovery Teaching

*Trade · Suth · Historical coverage: 943–1248 AFR*

Teachers demonstrate grafts using local rootstocks and bounded water. Reconstruction donors supply prestigious imported varieties poorly suited to damaged channels. Gardeners request useful materials instead, turning gratitude into a negotiation over what assistance can actually accomplish.

The trade depends upon tools, training, reliable materials, and people who perform work its public account barely mentions. A skilled craftsperson can improve a process, yet every batch still requires hands and a season of available materials. Output remains bounded by fuel, animals, weather, and the hands available to repair equipment. Prices carry the history of these constraints into households far from the workshop. A commercial undertaking that survives its maker can preserve useful cooperation or prevent successors from adapting to changed conditions. The present negotiation concerns how to retain knowledge while releasing an obligation nobody can now perform.

This belongs to The Judges Who Walk Between Gardens. Suth’s garden communes elected judges rather than inheriting a sovereign. In 943 a physician reformer sought to reduce the separate courts maintained by armed households and funerary custodians. His ally withdrew support when those institutions threatened revolt. Traveling musicians carried arguments between valleys, blending older measures with instruments learned in exile. An outside authority later occupied the eastern gardens while the western communes retained a different charter. When a public vote ended occupation, departing forces destroyed channels and records. Returning households therefore faced reconstruction before constitutional celebration. The elected judges now walk between settlements to hear claims, because neither the old exemptions nor the new vote can make water flow through a broken canal.

In the connected record, [The Servants’ Election Petition](b020-the-servants-election-petition.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Coerced Transfer Review](b020-the-coerced-transfer-review.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how elected courts can rebuild channels while deciding which occupation-era transactions were coerced and which present households depend upon them.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
