# The Returning Refrain

*Phenomenon · Suth · Historical coverage: 982–1248 AFR*

Wind moving through an opened stair produces the rhythm of a famous refrain. Musicians disagree about the similarity. Masons reproduce it with measured airflow, showing that a natural mechanism can sustain meaningful remembrance without proving the missing singers are speaking.

Witnesses distinguish an observed effect from their explanation for it. Repetition under comparable conditions is valuable evidence; a striking story told after its alleged cause becomes famous is weaker evidence. Masons, gardeners, and boat crews often recognize patterns an interpreter overlooks. Their explanation may reduce the immediate danger without settling whether an undertaking contributed to it. The basin has no institution capable of issuing a final answer for every manifestation. Communities record the conditions under which the effect occurs, then negotiate a response whose costs they can actually sustain.

This belongs to The Steps That Carry a Song. Suth’s burial terraces expanded for centuries, accommodating rulers, gardeners, pilgrims, and people denied formal household graves. In 982 a valley campaign classified several gardening communities as supporters of an armed revolt and destroyed their water records. Survivors carried names through sung measures adapted from an older harvesting repertoire. Runners relayed the verses across terraces, sometimes abandoning a timed contest to support an exhausted companion. Later courts trusted official burial inscriptions more than the songs. Comparing the two revealed that prestigious monuments had reused stone from destroyed communal channels. The present dispute concerns testimony embedded in performance and material, neither of which supplies an easy complete account.

In the connected record, [Terrace Wall Grafting](b005-terrace-wall-grafting.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Testimony in Measure Rule](b005-the-testimony-in-measure-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: who must maintain the terraces while descendants seek acknowledgment of the gardens their monuments displaced.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
