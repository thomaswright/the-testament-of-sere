# The Bridge of Mercies

*Place · Vey · Historical coverage: 774–1248 AFR*

The Bridge of Mercies spans the Vey just above the point where fresh water begins to taste faintly of salt. Its name is older than the murdered witness Maren. Builders in 774 promised that nobody pursued for an unpaid funeral fee would be arrested between the bridge's four boundary stones. It was a small sanctuary, intended to let bereaved households reach the riverside market and earn enough to bury their dead. The original promise did not protect fugitives accused of violence.

After Maren was killed here in 776, her daughters asked the council to spare the assassin. They did not argue that he was innocent. They argued that executing him beneath the bridge would give his unfinished undertaking a permanent claim to the same water as their mother's. The council rejected their reasoning, moved the execution to a dry yard, and congratulated itself on having solved both justice and hydraulics. Three months later the executioner's household began hearing Maren's pouring song from its empty jars. Nobody has established whether this was an ancestral effect, an act of intimidation, or a story invented after the fact.

Travelers now encounter competing memorials. The eastern parapet records the council's judgment. The western records the daughters' petition. A third inscription, repeatedly scratched off and restored, lists the kitchen workers who fed people waiting for both proceedings. These accounts do not agree about mercy, but none can be removed without damaging the bridge's masonry.

In the present year, the sanctuary protects debtors from twelve different funerary courts. Wardens admit petitioners in groups because the structure cannot bear a crowd. Its toll collector is elected by people who have used the sanctuary, giving indebted households an unusual public office. She spends most of her time arranging repairs. The bridge's famous history attracts generous donations for inscriptions and almost none for mortar. If its central arch fails, the basin will lose both a crossing and a legal interval in which people can become something other than the sum of their debts.
