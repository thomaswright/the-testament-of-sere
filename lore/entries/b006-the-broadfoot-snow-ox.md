# The Broadfoot Snow Ox

*Creature · Namar · Historical coverage: 667–1248 AFR*

Broadfoot oxen cross crusted snow but overheat on sheltered descents. Officers ordered them kept in heavy covers for a royal display, killing more animals than the storm did. Herdsmen’s descendants still remove ornamental coverings before entering the lower pass.

Reports describe a living creature before they describe a supernatural emblem. It needs food, shelter, and a place to breed, and it reacts to disturbances that witnesses may misunderstand. A keeper who recognizes those needs can often prevent the encounter later remembered as a curse. The animal cannot be made into an obedient instrument of ancestral power. Where an undertaking changes its habitat, the effect is indirect and costly to other inhabitants. Stories that treat its behavior as a moral verdict obscure both ordinary suffering and the human decisions that altered the site.

This belongs to The Feast of the Unawakened Heir. For fifty-three years, King Hevren expanded mountain fortresses using labor bound through inherited household pledges. His numerous descendants occupied competing commands. At a private feast in 667, nine senior relatives died and an injured heir was proclaimed sovereign without awakening. A surviving uncle ordered a winter crossing to secure the southern granaries before rival claimants could respond. Pack herds suffered terribly and the army reached its objective unable to carry food home. A satirist later composed three contradictory succession plays. Court readers preserve only the version flattering their patron; caravan households preserve the versions in which servants, animals, and weather determine the outcome.

In the connected record, [The Uncertain King’s Crossing](b006-the-uncertain-king-s-crossing.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Sleeping Hand Limit](b006-the-sleeping-hand-limit.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the crown’s descendants can release coerced labor claims without first agreeing which predecessor possessed the authority to make them.

The Namar passes join pasture settlements to caravan markets. Pack animals carry nearly everything that cannot be made from local stone, wool, or timber. Snow closes crossings irregularly, and a route that is short on a map may cost more food than a longer sheltered path. Hospices preserve undertakings made by travelers who never returned. Their custodians cannot assume that a visitor belongs to one sovereign or one witness tradition. A workable agreement must specify which season, which spring, and which people it protects, rather than claiming a universal welcome the stores cannot support.
