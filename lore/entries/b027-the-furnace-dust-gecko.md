# The Furnace Dust Gecko

*Creature · Keth · Historical coverage: 1181–1248 AFR*

Geckos hunt insects near warm workshop walls and leave when fuel smoke thickens. Workers use their disappearance to prompt ventilation checks. Patrons call it an omen of disloyalty, displacing an unsafe condition onto a politically useful accusation.

Reports describe a living creature before they describe a supernatural emblem. It needs food, shelter, and a place to breed, and it reacts to disturbances that witnesses may misunderstand. A keeper who recognizes those needs can often prevent the encounter later remembered as a curse. The animal cannot be made into an obedient instrument of ancestral power. Where an undertaking changes its habitat, the effect is indirect and costly to other inhabitants. Stories that treat its behavior as a moral verdict obscure both ordinary suffering and the human decisions that altered the site.

This belongs to The Sisters and the Furnace Sky. Two sisters rose through family-taught court games and challenged unequal prizes awarded to women. In 1181 a scholar excluded by inherited rank compared the colors of heated minerals, connecting visible changes to conditions rather than moral qualities. His work unsettled interpreters who assigned households celestial temperaments. A decade of assassinations, alliance reversals, and collapsing dynasties brought repeated campaigns into the northern shelves. Temporary truces displaced civilians without securing their return. The sisters’ touring courts became meeting places for injured and exiled players. Their public achievements could not stop war, but their practical demands for equal provision gave displaced households a vocabulary less dependent on whichever ruler currently controlled the furnace observatory.

In the connected record, [The Truce Return Path](b027-the-truce-return-path.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Equal Court Provision](b027-the-equal-court-provision.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether equal access agreements can survive military requisition of courts, fuel, and the water used to witness them.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
