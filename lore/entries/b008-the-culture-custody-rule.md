# The Culture Custody Rule

*Law · Orun · Historical coverage: 537–1248 AFR*

Custody follows documented feeding and maintenance of a culture, not a patron’s purchase of its jar. Courts can recognize shared keepers. The rule prevents a collector from taking the vessel while leaving households responsible for the continuing obligation attached to its contents.

The rule’s force depends on a stated scope. It governs named acts and claimants, rather than every future disagreement that resembles its original case. Courts have nevertheless tried to extend it, usually when a narrow precedent would benefit a powerful applicant. Opponents do not have to deny ancestor law to challenge that extension. They can ask which promise was actually made, who witnessed it, and whether the required observance remains possible. The difference between a valid undertaking and a convenient assertion of authority is central to the current dispute.

This belongs to The Measure of Living Bread. Orun’s grain towns supplied a distant court through assessed harvest deliveries. In 537, a returning exile founded a public assembly and hired a movement teacher to make its ceremonies legible to crowded quays. The teacher adapted milling, lifting, and kneading gestures into elaborate dances. Workshop experimenters meanwhile discovered that strained bread cultures could continue transforming a mixture after visible yeast sediment was removed. Officials called the process proof that ancestor power inhabited every loaf and tried to tax the cultures. Bakers compared batches and demonstrated that preparation mattered. The assembly’s leader later surrendered local authority to a wider union, leaving schools, granaries, and kitchens to negotiate the consequences of a political unity they had celebrated.

In the connected record, [Uren of the Returned Assembly](b008-uren-of-the-returned-assembly.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Culture Custody Rule](b008-the-culture-custody-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether bread cultures and harvest teaching belong to the households maintaining them or to the authority that sponsors their public performances.

The Orun channels shift across a landscape whose legal descriptions often name vanished banks. Fishers, reed workers, and gardeners share access without sharing a single account of ownership. Transport depends on shallow boats and pilots who read changes in sediment. Salt reaches some plots well before neighboring plots, making an apparently equal assessment unfair in practice. An ancestral claim can survive a change in the channel, but its observance may become impossible at the old site. Communities must decide whether to move the observance, compensate its claimants, or leave the affected water unused.
