# The Wax Faces

*Artifact tradition · Vey · Historical coverage: 783–1248 AFR*

Wax faces are palm-sized seal impressions used to borrow an absent person's recognized household identity. They contain no portrait. The name arose from a witness's complaint that a stamp had begun serving as a face at the court door. During the Unannounced War, travel became difficult for people whose original witnesses lived behind an opposing coalition's checkpoints. Brokers offered wax impressions accompanied by memorized family details. A borrowed identity could get a person through a gate, onto a ferry, or into a kitchen where an unrecognized stranger would be refused.

The practice did not create a new ancestral undertaking. It deceived living officials about which undertaking already applied. Brokers therefore preferred identities whose owners had disappeared but had not been legally declared dead. The missing person's unresolved status became a commodity. Relatives sometimes cooperated to support escape; sometimes they discovered that a vanished child or parent had been assigned to several unrelated clients.

Wax was both useful and treacherous. Heat softened a credential at precisely the season when travelers most needed water. Cheap impressions trapped hairs and fibers that could expose the broker's workshop. A border clerk who learned to recognize those fibers became more effective than a soldier at finding fugitives. One surviving case records a household buying its own confiscated identity back from the clerk, who then testified to its authenticity for an additional fee.

Vesh collects impressions only when their ownership can be documented. He stores them in shallow clay trays, each beside a note distinguishing the original name holder from known borrowers. Families object when a borrower's escape story becomes better known than the disappeared person's life. The collection has therefore acquired two separate indexes. In 1248, officials propose destroying the seals to prevent renewed fraud. Archivists counter that the seals show how courts manufactured vulnerability and how ordinary people navigated it. Both concerns are credible. A solution may involve keeping accurate dry copies while marking the surviving wax visibly enough that it can no longer serve as a useful credential.
