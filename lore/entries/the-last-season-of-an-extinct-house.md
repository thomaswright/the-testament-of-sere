# The Last Season of an Extinct House

*Practice · Suth · Historical coverage: 180–1248 AFR*

An extinct household can retain a valid undertaking without leaving a recognized person able to consent to its revision. The Last Season practice addresses that difficulty through a deliberately narrow substitute. Custodians identify the promised act, the surviving benefit, and the people currently bearing its cost. They appoint a temporary recipient for one season, witnessed by households with conflicting interests. Successful provision does not make the substitute the extinct family’s heir in every other matter.

The oldest documented case concerns a garden pledged to supply fruit for a hospice whose founders died without recognized descendants. Later owners maintained the delivery but could not change a route made dangerous by a collapsed channel. An interpreter demanded continuing obedience until a true heir appeared. Gardeners and patients instead chose one care worker to receive the fruit through a safer landing for a single harvest. Nothing destructive followed, and the hospice’s records show the intended provision continued.

The practice does not prove the dead approve whatever living people decide. Failed substitutions are recorded too. A wealthy claimant once appointed his own servant and diverted a portion into private stores; the affected spring lost usable water until the original amount was restored. The inquiry established a specific failure of provision, not a general right for the claimant to condemn unrelated households.

Some courts reject Last Season because it admits uncertainty about the recipient. Others abuse it by declaring inconvenient houses extinct before checking migrants, unrecognized children, and adopted kin. Vesh’s methods are useful safeguards: absence in one register must not become a convenient conclusion about a whole person’s life. A substitute is accepted only after documented inquiry and must remain answerable to people who might contradict it.

In 1248 the practice gives several doubly held people a possible route to undivided movement. It also supports repairs where an old agreement’s purpose survives but its original form cannot. The season’s ending matters. Custodians must review what happened rather than let temporary authority quietly become inherited jurisdiction. Its hope is neither universal absolution nor endless submission: a specific useful promise may be completed or carried forward in a form the living can actually sustain.
