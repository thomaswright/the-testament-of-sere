# The Peace Table Grain Cup

*Artifact · Suth · Historical coverage: 779–1248 AFR*

The cup measures food supplied per delegate and exposes expensive entourages. Patrons call counting inhospitable. Hosts explain that accounting makes continued welcome possible, preventing a prestigious meeting from exhausting the households whose generosity sustains it.

Material inspection places limits on the object’s legend. Repairs, wear, and changes in custody can establish that it was used repeatedly without proving every story attached to it. Its value is relational: somebody can authenticate it, somebody needs it for an observance, and somebody may suffer if it is removed. An expensive replacement would not automatically inherit those relations. Custodians therefore record loans and repairs as carefully as ceremonial appearances. A collector who purchases the object but ignores its attached undertaking may own something that cannot safely be taken home.

This belongs to The Epic and the Unoccupied Garden. An upland founder’s life survived through performed epic alongside sparse independent records. Later households used the epic to justify incompatible claims to a fertile valley. In 779 a negotiator established a garden zone from which armed officers withdrew for talks, hoping protected meeting space would end recurring conflict. Outside coalitions continued contesting its borders and access. A naval battle far downstream redirected trade, weakening sponsors who had supplied the negotiations and strengthening new patrons. The peace zone became both a genuine refuge and a place where rivals accumulated influence. Present gardeners compare heroic memory, signed terms, and changed supply routes to preserve talks without pretending withdrawal of one force made the valley free of coercion.

In the connected record, [The Epic’s Unchosen Listener](b039-the-epic-s-unchosen-listener.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Resident Within Neutrality](b039-the-resident-within-neutrality.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the meeting garden can remain protected when the trade revenues supporting its food and repairs now pass through rival downstream hands.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
