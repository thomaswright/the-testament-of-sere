# Linen for Stone

*Trade · Keth · Historical coverage: 846–1248 AFR*

Making supports for peeled sheets gives widowed spinners a new market. Dealers try to secure perpetual prices through ancestral contracts. The spinners offer seasonal commitments instead, since the amount of usable stone changes with every quarry seam.

The trade depends upon tools, training, reliable materials, and people who perform work its public account barely mentions. A skilled craftsperson can improve a process, yet every batch still requires hands and a season of available materials. Output remains bounded by fuel, animals, weather, and the hands available to repair equipment. Prices carry the history of these constraints into households far from the workshop. A commercial undertaking that survives its maker can preserve useful cooperation or prevent successors from adapting to changed conditions. The present negotiation concerns how to retain knowledge while releasing an obligation nobody can now perform.

This belongs to The Crown of Peeled Stone. In 846, Commander Darr declared himself emperor of the chalk shelves and borrowed heavily to furnish a coronation cistern. His experimenter discovered that repeatedly peeling a dark stone produced sheets sensitive to wet fingerprints. Darr used the sheets to stage an assembly approving his annexation of neighboring terraces. Armed attendants controlled admission, and absent households were counted as consenting. After his fall in 871, the court identified a picturesque fortress as the rebels’ last refuge. Workmen later found the real shelter beneath a humble plaster yard, where fingerprints preserved evidence of people excluded from the imperial vote. The discovery endangered both victorious families and the fallen ruler’s heirs.

In the connected record, [The Refuge Compensation Hearing](b002-the-refuge-compensation-hearing.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Unpressed Consent Rule](b002-the-unpressed-consent-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether authenticated exclusion can revise ownership without depriving present residents of the cisterns they maintain.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
