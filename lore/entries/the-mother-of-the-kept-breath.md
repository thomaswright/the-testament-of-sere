# The Mother of the Kept Breath

*Disputed tradition · Avel · Historical coverage: Before 0–1248 AFR*

Avel’s oldest account of ancestor law concerns Ilem, a woman waiting beside a flooded landing for children who could not cross. She promised to keep a cup of air beneath an overturned bowl until they arrived. The children were real people in some versions and the names of unborn islands in others. Ilem died before the crossing. When survivors lifted the bowl, a single breath remained beneath it and disturbed the water after every ordinary wind had ceased.

No surviving object authenticates the story. The overturned bowl shown at three island shrines cannot belong to all three versions, and none has a reliable custody chain reaching an uncounted age. Their keepers generally acknowledge this. They preserve the vessels as teaching objects, not as proof that all basin law derives from one woman or one island. Mainland collectors are less cautious and have repeatedly offered money for the most ancient-looking example.

The tradition explains a limit more often than an origin. Ilem keeps one breath for particular children, not endless air for everyone. She does not escape death or appoint her descendants rulers of the sea. Her act remains incomplete until its intended recipients arrive or credible successors agree upon another use. Young listeners sometimes find that ending disappointing. Teachers ask whether a promise becomes less valuable because it cannot repair the whole world.

A darker version says the breath never belonged to Ilem. She borrowed it from something already waiting beneath the landing, and human undertakings are attempts to account for that older loan. Reciters disagree about whether the something was a drowned household, a beast without a mouth, or simply the water’s unclaimed pressure. None of those descriptions has been confirmed.

The tradition continues in ordinary hospitality. Hosts place one empty cup for an expected traveler and remove it after an explicitly stated interval. They avoid promising a universal welcome their stores could not support. Teachers of the progressive coastal lessons use the story without requiring children to believe it. In 1248 its strongest shared teaching is modest: remembrance may keep an act available, but the living must still decide how to complete it without turning one finite kindness into a claim upon everyone who comes later.
