# The Lantern of Borrowed Illumination

*Artifact · Vey · Historical coverage: 1176–1248 AFR*

Its glass panels combine work from several households. A family claims ancestral ownership of the whole technique. Makers identify specific contributions, distinguishing a useful inherited object from a claim to control every future use of colored light.

Material inspection places limits on the object’s legend. Repairs, wear, and changes in custody can establish that it was used repeatedly without proving every story attached to it. Its value is relational: somebody can authenticate it, somebody needs it for an observance, and somebody may suffer if it is removed. An expensive replacement would not automatically inherit those relations. Custodians therefore record loans and repairs as carefully as ceremonial appearances. A collector who purchases the object but ignores its attached undertaking may own something that cannot safely be taken home.

This belongs to The Theater of Linked Doors. After a long-serving magistrate withdrew in 1176, rival councils shared offices without agreeing who could issue orders. A traveling dramatist used colored lanterns and hinged scenery to stage judgments between living witnesses and dead claimants. Another performer, trained abroad, brought stories about inherited knowledge denied to younger relatives. Readers connected their manuscript copies through explicit page references, allowing an account in one town to point to evidence held in another. Patrols seized central catalogs but could not erase copies already dispersed. The network preserved testimony and also exposed vulnerable households when careless references revealed their locations. Its keepers now negotiate the difference between public evidence and information that enables coercion.

In the connected record, [The Reference Spoken Twice](b015-the-reference-spoken-twice.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Withheld Location Clause](b015-the-withheld-location-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: which references should remain public when making testimony accessible can also identify people a patrol wants to seize.

Vey households measure wealth partly in dependable access to a public landing. Their markets exchange grain, lamp oil, glazed jars, and legal testimony. A witness can authenticate a transaction but cannot provide the labor needed to fulfill it. Seasonal crews clean the same embankments on which courts announce judgments, and a decision that ignores those crews soon meets an expensive physical limit. Flood deposits preserve small objects while destroying the arrangement that once made their meaning obvious. Recovering an object is therefore easier than establishing what anybody was entitled to do with it.
