# The Names Before Migration

*Text · Suth · Historical coverage: 631–1248 AFR*

The register follows changing settlement names without equating them with unchanged populations. It includes marriages and departures. Revival leaders who seek one continuous ancestral people must confront the ordinary movements that made their present community possible.

Copies disagree in ways that matter. A shortened phrase can remove a condition, and a copied date can imply that a later amendment belonged to the original undertaking. Dry writing preserves evidence but cannot make an oath binding by itself. Readers must distinguish a record of consent from a writer’s assertion that consent occurred. Marginal notes are sometimes the only surviving testimony of people excluded from the formal version. Establishing a trustworthy reading therefore depends on comparing custody, material, and ordinary expenses, as well as elegant arguments about wording.

This belongs to The Women Who Named the Hidden Country. Garden migrants carried a coastal place name inland until it became the name of several distinct settlements. Later patrons suppressed their administrative institutions but could not erase household speech. In 631 a materials negotiator organized scarce timber and metal during war, then sought agreements with former enemies. He was murdered amid accusations about his outsider ancestry. Women gathering in garden parlors wrote elaborate wonder tales whose hidden countries unsettled the courts’ official map of belonging. A language revival collected their work beside older oral accounts, sometimes mistaking polished literary invention for unchanged folklore. Present readers preserve both forms and question which histories were chosen to make a people appear uniform after migration.

In the connected record, [The Unpromised Shadow](b031-the-unpromised-shadow.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Speech Without Descent Test](b031-the-speech-without-descent-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether revived language institutions can welcome their actual mixed descendants without replacing old exclusion with a newly polished ancestry test.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
