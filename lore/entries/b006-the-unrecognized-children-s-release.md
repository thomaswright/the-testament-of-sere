# The Unrecognized Children’s Release

*Dispute · Namar · Historical coverage: 667–1248 AFR*

Unrecognized descendants offer to surrender disputed royal claims in exchange for ending guard bondage. Established branches object that this acknowledges their standing. Workers insist the release cannot depend on preserving the same genealogy that erased half its witnesses.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The Feast of the Unawakened Heir. For fifty-three years, King Hevren expanded mountain fortresses using labor bound through inherited household pledges. His numerous descendants occupied competing commands. At a private feast in 667, nine senior relatives died and an injured heir was proclaimed sovereign without awakening. A surviving uncle ordered a winter crossing to secure the southern granaries before rival claimants could respond. Pack herds suffered terribly and the army reached its objective unable to carry food home. A satirist later composed three contradictory succession plays. Court readers preserve only the version flattering their patron; caravan households preserve the versions in which servants, animals, and weather determine the outcome.

In the connected record, [Hevren of the Many Courtyards](b006-hevren-of-the-many-courtyards.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Sleeping Hand Limit](b006-the-sleeping-hand-limit.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the crown’s descendants can release coerced labor claims without first agreeing which predecessor possessed the authority to make them.

The Namar passes join pasture settlements to caravan markets. Pack animals carry nearly everything that cannot be made from local stone, wool, or timber. Snow closes crossings irregularly, and a route that is short on a map may cost more food than a longer sheltered path. Hospices preserve undertakings made by travelers who never returned. Their custodians cannot assume that a visitor belongs to one sovereign or one witness tradition. A workable agreement must specify which season, which spring, and which people it protects, rather than claiming a universal welcome the stores cannot support.
