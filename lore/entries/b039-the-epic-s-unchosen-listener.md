# The Epic’s Unchosen Listener

*Custom · Suth · Historical coverage: 779–1248 AFR*

Listeners may decline a version without losing access to garden meals. Claimant rulers object that refusal insults ancestral authority. The practice keeps performance from manufacturing consent, especially where an epic is being used to support disputed title.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to The Epic and the Unoccupied Garden. An upland founder’s life survived through performed epic alongside sparse independent records. Later households used the epic to justify incompatible claims to a fertile valley. In 779 a negotiator established a garden zone from which armed officers withdrew for talks, hoping protected meeting space would end recurring conflict. Outside coalitions continued contesting its borders and access. A naval battle far downstream redirected trade, weakening sponsors who had supplied the negotiations and strengthening new patrons. The peace zone became both a genuine refuge and a place where rivals accumulated influence. Present gardeners compare heroic memory, signed terms, and changed supply routes to preserve talks without pretending withdrawal of one force made the valley free of coercion.

In the connected record, [The Naval Toll’s Inland Costs](b039-the-naval-toll-s-inland-costs.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Resident Within Neutrality](b039-the-resident-within-neutrality.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the meeting garden can remain protected when the trade revenues supporting its food and repairs now pass through rival downstream hands.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
