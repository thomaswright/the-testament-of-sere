# The Separate Restitution Clause

*Law · Vey · Historical coverage: 208–1248 AFR*

The clause distinguishes pardon, repentance, and return of property. None automatically performs the others. Patrons support its moral language but oppose inspection of their rents. Petitioners now request separate written decisions for each issue so one favorable phrase cannot conceal two refusals.

The rule’s force depends on a stated scope. It governs named acts and claimants, rather than every future disagreement that resembles its original case. Courts have nevertheless tried to extend it, usually when a narrow precedent would benefit a powerful applicant. Opponents do not have to deny ancestor law to challenge that extension. They can ask which promise was actually made, who witnessed it, and whether the required observance remains possible. The difference between a valid undertaking and a convenient assertion of authority is central to the current dispute.

This belongs to The City Beneath the Recitation. Sere’s oldest inland temple was repeatedly rebuilt as river silt raised the surrounding ground. Its custodians recited a single founding poem, although repairs preserved portions of older and contradictory versions. In 903, suspicion of coastal ancestry led Vey’s council to confine thousands of residents in dry compounds. Their former neighbors acquired abandoned workshops. A household prayer movement later promised release from inherited guilt through a simple declaration made beside water. A scholar comparing temple recitations argued that neither the city’s origin nor its wrongdoing could be reduced to one speaker and one cleansing act. His evidence joined the displaced families’ claims to foundations the council called sacred.

In the connected record, [Pera of the Kitchen Meetings](b004-pera-of-the-kitchen-meetings.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Separate Restitution Clause](b004-the-separate-restitution-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether a public act of repentance will restore workshop access or merely authorize the present owners to feel absolved.

Vey households measure wealth partly in dependable access to a public landing. Their markets exchange grain, lamp oil, glazed jars, and legal testimony. A witness can authenticate a transaction but cannot provide the labor needed to fulfill it. Seasonal crews clean the same embankments on which courts announce judgments, and a decision that ignores those crews soon meets an expensive physical limit. Flood deposits preserve small objects while destroying the arrangement that once made their meaning obvious. Recovering an object is therefore easier than establishing what anybody was entitled to do with it.
