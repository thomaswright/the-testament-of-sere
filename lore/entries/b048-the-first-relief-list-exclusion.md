# The First Relief List Exclusion

*Event · Talassar · Historical coverage: 1232–1248 AFR*

Unregistered households were omitted despite shelter loss. Officials called the omission a verification problem. Kitchen records established actual meals and occupants, creating narrow reliable evidence without requiring displaced claimants to recover documents buried in the same collapsed rooms.

The event survives through records made for different purposes. A household remembers hunger; a court records jurisdiction; a custodian records the interruption of an observance. Their dates can be reconciled without making their explanations identical. Later commemorations often select a decisive moment because a ceremony needs a beginning and an end. The consequences were less orderly, traveling through marriages, prices, and people who moved away. An honest account follows those consequences far enough to distinguish a temporary outcome from an agreement that still constrains the present.

This belongs to The Note Beneath the Broken Mint. The mint quarter shook in 1232, collapsing crowded housing and damaging courts, stores, and care buildings. Repeated aftershocks made return dangerous. Debt, unequal construction, and earlier exclusion worsened harm; the shaking itself supplied no moral judgment. A singer famous for a brilliant high register but limited elsewhere used carefully chosen notes to help searchers detect replies through rubble. An aging surveillance administrator sought control of relief lists, while independent kitchens compared coins, claims, and actual food. The disaster revealed several money forms—metal, shells, promised grain, and account entries—whose usefulness differed when stores and witnesses disappeared. Present rebuilding negotiations preserve debt evidence while asking which obligations can still be fulfilled without sacrificing households to figures detached from available resources.

In the connected record, [The Singer’s Limited Pitch Board](b048-the-singer-s-limited-pitch-board.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Available Goods Relief Test](b048-the-available-goods-relief-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how rebuilding and old accounts can proceed when recorded wealth exceeds accessible food, shelter, and the skilled labor needed to restore them.

Talassar combines salt marshes, sheltered boat towns, and orchards whose irrigation depends on tidal timing. Merchants trade durable claims alongside perishable cargo, although only an actual witnessed undertaking has force after death. A repaired boat, a borrowed name, and a neglected drain can each determine whether a contract remains practical. Houses that prosper from distant obligations are exposed to local maintenance costs they would prefer to call somebody else’s problem. Seasonal kitchens and landing crews keep accounts that often describe the region more accurately than its ceremonial histories of uninterrupted family success.
