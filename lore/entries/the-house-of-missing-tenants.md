# The House of Missing Tenants

*Place · Vey · Historical coverage: 780–1248 AFR*

The House of Missing Tenants occupies a narrow plot between a dyer's yard and a disused court stair. From the street it appears to contain four rented rooms. Behind the upper corridor are nine additional chambers, reached through doors painted to resemble shelving. These were not built as secret prisons. They began as inexpensive storage spaces and acquired beds when the Unannounced War made travel papers more valuable than adequate shelter. The public rental roll continued describing them as cupboards.

Tenants paid for a room and the use of a name recognized by a local witness. Some were escaping the Green Tithe's added labor claims. Others were merchants avoiding inspection fees, soldiers deserting both coalitions, or families whose legitimate documents had been destroyed. The house's keeper did not distinguish among them until their payments failed. Then the same secrecy that sheltered a person could make that person easy to sell.

The house was seized in 794 after a baker complained that its official population could not account for the bread it purchased. The investigation established unregistered lodging but declined to identify individual tenants. Several councilors had relatives who used the chambers, and the rent book contained payments from officers already honored as liberators. The keeper was fined. The hidden rooms were sealed, allowing the city to punish a building while protecting its clients.

Vesh reopened one chamber in 1215 with a key inherited from his father. Since then, volunteers have searched cracks in the plaster for bills, scraps of clothing, and tally ends. Their discoveries rarely identify a whole person; more often they join two previously disconnected facts. The current owner charges no rent for the research room but rents the other visible rooms at market rates. People still live above the sealed chambers, cook supper, argue about washing, and descend the same stairs as visitors carrying flowers for unnamed victims. The coexistence troubles memorial committees. It also keeps the roof repaired, which their solemn proposals have repeatedly failed to do.
