# The Terraces of the Second Foot

*Necropolis · Keth · Historical coverage: 349–1248 AFR*

The burial terraces face east because the old shelf court promised each named grave the first water carried from the morning cistern. This was once a small undertaking: twenty-seven graves, twenty-seven cups, one household assigned to each. The court subsequently added soldiers, servants, and relatives by declaring them members of those households. When its dominion broke apart in 349, three successor offices inherited different lists. They continued pouring water into the same stone mouths.

The terraces did not rise from the ground. Their walls remained where the masons built them. What changed was the descent. A bearer walking down after the morning observance felt a second footfall beside the first, close enough to seem their own and always half a pace late. Some bearers began putting one foot on the step above and the other on the step below, unable to complete either movement. The earliest account describes a woman holding a cup for seven hours while her brother cut its leather sling. Releasing the cup let her sit; returning to the staircase restored the divided act.

The present route uses wooden crossings between levels. Custodians pour each lineage's water on separate mornings, allowing the lower cistern to recover between them. This has reduced the divided movements without establishing which successor office possesses the better claim. Anyone visiting the terraces can see the expense: worn planks, ropes hung to dry, and garden rows shortened because burial water still has to come from somewhere. The poor dead receive no more water by becoming numerous in a court's account.

Lonsha's salt readers have mapped branching deposits around the old stone mouths. Their drawings suggest three different channels feed two of the graves claimed by all three lists. They have requested permission to expose the junction. One office fears damage to the observance; another fears evidence that would weaken its genealogy. The third has offered money for an investigation whose conclusion it would approve in advance. The readers refused that condition.

In 1248, an unclaimed infant's grave remains outside the terraces beneath a repaired pear tree. Its neighbors maintain it without asserting that affection supplies ancestral title. People sometimes call that grave the only free place on the shelf. The phrase is too easy. The tree needs pruning, the marker needs shelter, and someone carries water even there. Yet the care can still be offered for one season and renewed by choice. The terrace bearers stop beside it before entering the old obligations, both feet on the same earth.
