# Roadside Soup Provisioning

*Trade · Orun · Historical coverage: 873–1248 AFR*

Cooks adapt portions to arrivals and available fuel, documenting shortages rather than pretending every traveler was fed. Their honest accounts support later compensation. Ceremonial claims of limitless generosity would make those specific obligations disappear.

The trade depends upon tools, training, reliable materials, and people who perform work its public account barely mentions. A skilled craftsperson can improve a process, yet every batch still requires hands and a season of available materials. Output remains bounded by fuel, animals, weather, and the hands available to repair equipment. Prices carry the history of these constraints into households far from the workshop. A commercial undertaking that survives its maker can preserve useful cooperation or prevent successors from adapting to changed conditions. The present negotiation concerns how to retain knowledge while releasing an obligation nobody can now perform.

This belongs to The Diary of the Fog Muster. A provincial commander pursued raiders into a narrow lakeside road during morning mist, convinced that delay would damage his reputation. His force was trapped between water and hidden slopes. Survivors returned to towns where a separate levy attempt had triggered running confrontations along several roads. A household diarist recorded provisions, embarrassment, conflicting rumors, and the gap between expected courage and actual fear. A comic performer later adapted the diary’s domestic quarrels. Authorities preferred a grand account of one awakening people, but the small records reveal uneven preparation and changing choices. They also preserve meals, messages, and repairs that enabled resistance more reliably than the flattering language of destiny.

In the connected record, [The Courage Levy Petition](b029-the-courage-levy-petition.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Levy’s Stated Purpose](b029-the-levy-s-stated-purpose.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how commemorations can honor real resistance without using its heroic image to impose another compulsory levy on the households that sustained it.

The Orun channels shift across a landscape whose legal descriptions often name vanished banks. Fishers, reed workers, and gardeners share access without sharing a single account of ownership. Transport depends on shallow boats and pilots who read changes in sediment. Salt reaches some plots well before neighboring plots, making an apparently equal assessment unfair in practice. An ancestral claim can survive a change in the channel, but its observance may become impossible at the old site. Communities must decide whether to move the observance, compensate its claimants, or leave the affected water unused.
