# The Tour’s Missing Towns

*Text · Talassar · Historical coverage: 1206–1248 AFR*

A booklet records repairs requested by landings omitted from the itinerary. It includes places that declined the visit deliberately. This distinction prevents a useful pattern of unequal attention from becoming an unsupported claim that every omission was imposed.

Copies disagree in ways that matter. A shortened phrase can remove a condition, and a copied date can imply that a later amendment belonged to the original undertaking. Dry writing preserves evidence but cannot make an oath binding by itself. Readers must distinguish a record of consent from a writer’s assertion that consent occurred. Marginal notes are sometimes the only surviving testimony of people excluded from the formal version. Establishing a trustworthy reading therefore depends on comparing custody, material, and ordinary expenses, as well as elegant arguments about wording.

This belongs to The Tour of the Fragrant Ruins. In 1206, a retired basin governor toured southern landing towns to encourage reopening fragrant-bark trade. He held no formal office but his presence changed appointments and credit. A painter accompanying him produced grand images of restored prosperity, while a nearly motionless singer performed laments for workers lost on earlier voyages. Merchants circulated fantastic stories about the bark’s remote source to protect routes and prices. The tour helped revive genuine employment but also bypassed local review of old concessions. Present households compare painted abundance, songs of vulnerability, and actual cargo accounts to decide which promises were made and which audiences merely assumed from a powerful visitor’s approval.

In the connected record, [The Fragrant Warehouse Sleep](b018-the-fragrant-warehouse-sleep.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Signature Before the Praise](b018-the-signature-before-the-praise.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether renewed trade can preserve local oversight when the commitments were encouraged by an influential person who signed no accountable undertaking.

Talassar combines salt marshes, sheltered boat towns, and orchards whose irrigation depends on tidal timing. Merchants trade durable claims alongside perishable cargo, although only an actual witnessed undertaking has force after death. A repaired boat, a borrowed name, and a neglected drain can each determine whether a contract remains practical. Houses that prosper from distant obligations are exposed to local maintenance costs they would prefer to call somebody else’s problem. Seasonal kitchens and landing crews keep accounts that often describe the region more accurately than its ceremonial histories of uninterrupted family success.
