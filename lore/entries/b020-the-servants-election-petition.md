# The Servants’ Election Petition

*Dispute · Suth · Historical coverage: 943–1248 AFR*

Servants maintain communal channels but cannot elect the judges ruling upon their work. Their petition invokes contribution rather than a fictitious ancient charter. Commune leaders must decide whether self-government includes those whose labor makes the governing households viable.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The Judges Who Walk Between Gardens. Suth’s garden communes elected judges rather than inheriting a sovereign. In 943 a physician reformer sought to reduce the separate courts maintained by armed households and funerary custodians. His ally withdrew support when those institutions threatened revolt. Traveling musicians carried arguments between valleys, blending older measures with instruments learned in exile. An outside authority later occupied the eastern gardens while the western communes retained a different charter. When a public vote ended occupation, departing forces destroyed channels and records. Returning households therefore faced reconstruction before constitutional celebration. The elected judges now walk between settlements to hear claims, because neither the old exemptions nor the new vote can make water flow through a broken canal.

In the connected record, [Valen the Basin Physician](b020-valen-the-basin-physician.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Coerced Transfer Review](b020-the-coerced-transfer-review.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how elected courts can rebuild channels while deciding which occupation-era transactions were coerced and which present households depend upon them.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
