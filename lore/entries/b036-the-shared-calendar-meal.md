# The Shared Calendar Meal

*Custom · Keth · Historical coverage: 402–1248 AFR*

Households explain both sector dates before recording a new agreement. The custom prevents mismatched anniversaries becoming accusations of broken observance. Reconciliation begins through usable conversions rather than requiring one community’s remembered time to be erased.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to The Last Officer and the Eastern Wall. After the shelf empire dissolved in 349, one cistern city retained its officer, school, and provisioning offices. Neighbors called the officer a king, although his own documents refused the title. In 402 a conquering ruler adopted the name of a legendary ancient sovereign and took the city, promising a restored golden age. Later wars involved southern powers and outside specialists whose aid changed local dependency. An occupation divided the city behind a wall, making its eastern rooms a capital others refused to recognize. Weaving schools carried lessons across successive regimes. Their history preserves practical continuity without accepting every conqueror’s claim that an old name made the present order a reborn original.

In the connected record, [The Capitals Not Recognized](b036-the-capitals-not-recognized.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Successor’s Explicit Scope](b036-the-successor-s-explicit-scope.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether reunited city households can share cistern access while untangling obligations authenticated by offices whose legitimacy each successor disputed.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
