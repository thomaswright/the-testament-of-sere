# The Boot Account

*Document · Orun · Historical coverage: 611–1248 AFR*

The Boot Account is a provisioning leaf repaired with thin leather along its bottom edge. Its surviving entries document the settlers' final hungry weeks, including a pair of boots cut into fourteen portions. The leather repair was added later by a binder who did not know the page's subject. This accident has tempted generations of commentators to describe the document itself as a meal preserved in writing. The surviving leather cannot be dated closely enough to support that claim.

More consequential is the ration mark used for an unregistered servant. Official copies replace it with a blank, but the original mark appears beside net repairs, fish deliveries, and a loan of cooking pots. It identifies the person who negotiated an informal exchange with neighboring fishers while the overseer demanded recognition of his land grant. The leaf never supplies a name. Its evidence is nevertheless enough to show that the settlement survived partly through an agreement its leaders did not make and did not later acknowledge.

The Green Tithe's festivals display an enlarged reproduction with the servant's mark omitted. Curators say the omission makes the account easier to read. Reed households reply that readability is not the difficulty: recognizing the servant's contribution would undermine the faction's account of rescue as a transaction between merchants and legitimate founders alone. An accurate copy might support the descendants of fishers whose aid has never been included in the repayment calculation.

In 1248, Vesh is comparing the mark with pottery signs from the neighboring settlement's abandoned cooking shelter. A resemblance would establish a shared accounting practice, not necessarily an individual's identity. He has refused a patron's request to announce a recovered name before the comparison is complete. The leaf remains available for inspection behind an ordinary mesh screen, which protects it from sleeves and insects. Scholars have asked for a locked cabinet. Its custodian objects that the document has already spent too much of its history being kept safe from the people whose history it records.
