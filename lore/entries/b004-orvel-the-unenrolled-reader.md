# Orvel the Unenrolled Reader

*Person · Vey · Historical coverage: 208–1248 AFR*

Orvel registered as a comparison reader before the temple school recognized that occupation. Refused formal lectures, he copied masons’ marks instead. His pupils now control admission to a prestigious chair and are debating whether to repeat the exclusion that formed his method.

Later accounts make Orvel the Unenrolled Reader the explanation for an entire local change. The surviving record instead places this life among dependencies: an introduction supplied by another household, materials purchased on credit, and testimony given by people with reasons of their own. A biography that removes those dependencies becomes a useful legend and a poor guide to what happened. Descendants inherit evidence and expectations, rather than the ability to repeat an ancestor’s achievement. The practical question is which part of the achievement can be maintained by ordinary people after the celebrated individual is gone.

This belongs to The City Beneath the Recitation. Sere’s oldest inland temple was repeatedly rebuilt as river silt raised the surrounding ground. Its custodians recited a single founding poem, although repairs preserved portions of older and contradictory versions. In 903, suspicion of coastal ancestry led Vey’s council to confine thousands of residents in dry compounds. Their former neighbors acquired abandoned workshops. A household prayer movement later promised release from inherited guilt through a simple declaration made beside water. A scholar comparing temple recitations argued that neither the city’s origin nor its wrongdoing could be reduced to one speaker and one cleansing act. His evidence joined the displaced families’ claims to foundations the council called sacred.

In the connected record, [The Temple of Lower Steps](b004-the-temple-of-lower-steps.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Separate Restitution Clause](b004-the-separate-restitution-clause.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether a public act of repentance will restore workshop access or merely authorize the present owners to feel absolved.

Vey households measure wealth partly in dependable access to a public landing. Their markets exchange grain, lamp oil, glazed jars, and legal testimony. A witness can authenticate a transaction but cannot provide the labor needed to fulfill it. Seasonal crews clean the same embankments on which courts announce judgments, and a decision that ignores those crews soon meets an expensive physical limit. Flood deposits preserve small objects while destroying the arrangement that once made their meaning obvious. Recovering an object is therefore easier than establishing what anybody was entitled to do with it.
