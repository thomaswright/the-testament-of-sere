# The Bronze Water Seat

*Artifact · Keth · Historical coverage: 846–1248 AFR*

Darr’s ceremonial seat was hollow to reduce its cost. A seam leaks during public washing, exposing the economy beneath its imperial image. Conservators propose repairing it; creditors prefer the leak as evidence, while water carriers would rather sell the bronze for pipes.

Material inspection places limits on the object’s legend. Repairs, wear, and changes in custody can establish that it was used repeatedly without proving every story attached to it. Its value is relational: somebody can authenticate it, somebody needs it for an observance, and somebody may suffer if it is removed. An expensive replacement would not automatically inherit those relations. Custodians therefore record loans and repairs as carefully as ceremonial appearances. A collector who purchases the object but ignores its attached undertaking may own something that cannot safely be taken home.

This belongs to The Crown of Peeled Stone. In 846, Commander Darr declared himself emperor of the chalk shelves and borrowed heavily to furnish a coronation cistern. His experimenter discovered that repeatedly peeling a dark stone produced sheets sensitive to wet fingerprints. Darr used the sheets to stage an assembly approving his annexation of neighboring terraces. Armed attendants controlled admission, and absent households were counted as consenting. After his fall in 871, the court identified a picturesque fortress as the rebels’ last refuge. Workmen later found the real shelter beneath a humble plaster yard, where fingerprints preserved evidence of people excluded from the imperial vote. The discovery endangered both victorious families and the fallen ruler’s heirs.

In the connected record, [The Empty Stool Vigil](b002-the-empty-stool-vigil.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Unpressed Consent Rule](b002-the-unpressed-consent-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether authenticated exclusion can revise ownership without depriving present residents of the cisterns they maintain.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
