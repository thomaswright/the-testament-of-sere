# The Wall’s Unended Pressure

*Phenomenon · Keth · Historical coverage: 402–1248 AFR*

A sealed channel presses water against the old division after reunion. Masonry explains the blockage, but an unfinished access pledge complicates removal. Keepers seek consent and repairs together, addressing both material obstruction and the finite obligation attached to it.

Witnesses distinguish an observed effect from their explanation for it. Repetition under comparable conditions is valuable evidence; a striking story told after its alleged cause becomes famous is weaker evidence. Masons, gardeners, and boat crews often recognize patterns an interpreter overlooks. Their explanation may reduce the immediate danger without settling whether an undertaking contributed to it. The basin has no institution capable of issuing a final answer for every manifestation. Communities record the conditions under which the effect occurs, then negotiate a response whose costs they can actually sustain.

This belongs to The Last Officer and the Eastern Wall. After the shelf empire dissolved in 349, one cistern city retained its officer, school, and provisioning offices. Neighbors called the officer a king, although his own documents refused the title. In 402 a conquering ruler adopted the name of a legendary ancient sovereign and took the city, promising a restored golden age. Later wars involved southern powers and outside specialists whose aid changed local dependency. An occupation divided the city behind a wall, making its eastern rooms a capital others refused to recognize. Weaving schools carried lessons across successive regimes. Their history preserves practical continuity without accepting every conqueror’s claim that an old name made the present order a reborn original.

In the connected record, [Calendar Conversion Teaching](b036-calendar-conversion-teaching.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Successor’s Explicit Scope](b036-the-successor-s-explicit-scope.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether reunited city households can share cistern access while untangling obligations authenticated by offices whose legitimacy each successor disputed.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
