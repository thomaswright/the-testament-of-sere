# Hevren of the Many Courtyards

*Person · Namar · Historical coverage: 667–1248 AFR*

Hevren allocated a courtyard to each recognized branch of his household, then charged their residents for its maintenance. His long peace depended on continual fortress labor. Descendants invoke his protection while disagreeing about whether workers were ever entitled to leave.

Later accounts make Hevren of the Many Courtyards the explanation for an entire local change. The surviving record instead places this life among dependencies: an introduction supplied by another household, materials purchased on credit, and testimony given by people with reasons of their own. A biography that removes those dependencies becomes a useful legend and a poor guide to what happened. Descendants inherit evidence and expectations, rather than the ability to repeat an ancestor’s achievement. The practical question is which part of the achievement can be maintained by ordinary people after the celebrated individual is gone.

This belongs to The Feast of the Unawakened Heir. For fifty-three years, King Hevren expanded mountain fortresses using labor bound through inherited household pledges. His numerous descendants occupied competing commands. At a private feast in 667, nine senior relatives died and an injured heir was proclaimed sovereign without awakening. A surviving uncle ordered a winter crossing to secure the southern granaries before rival claimants could respond. Pack herds suffered terribly and the army reached its objective unable to carry food home. A satirist later composed three contradictory succession plays. Court readers preserve only the version flattering their patron; caravan households preserve the versions in which servants, animals, and weather determine the outcome.

In the connected record, [The Closed Feast Hall](b006-the-closed-feast-hall.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Sleeping Hand Limit](b006-the-sleeping-hand-limit.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the crown’s descendants can release coerced labor claims without first agreeing which predecessor possessed the authority to make them.

The Namar passes join pasture settlements to caravan markets. Pack animals carry nearly everything that cannot be made from local stone, wool, or timber. Snow closes crossings irregularly, and a route that is short on a map may cost more food than a longer sheltered path. Hospices preserve undertakings made by travelers who never returned. Their custodians cannot assume that a visitor belongs to one sovereign or one witness tradition. A workable agreement must specify which season, which spring, and which people it protects, rather than claiming a universal welcome the stores cannot support.
