# The Sign Explained Aloud

*Custom · Talassar · Historical coverage: 1123–1248 AFR*

Negotiators state the cloth’s intended meaning before crossing a boundary. The receiving guard repeats it before witnesses. This cumbersome custom prevents assumptions becoming convenient evidence and makes safe approach depend upon a recorded exchange rather than a universally understood symbol.

The custom is learned through participation rather than a single authoritative text. Households disagree about details while recognizing enough shared actions to cooperate. That flexibility can admit newcomers or allow influential families to redefine the practice in their own favor. Describing it as ancient settles neither possibility. Its practical test is whether a participant can question a demand without losing access to food, water, or witnesses. Local reformers seek finite observances whose costs can be recorded, so that reverence for predecessors does not become an unanswerable demand upon children.

This belongs to The Empty Cloth Negotiations. After several seasons of falling prices in 1123, Talassar merchants reduced wages while their stored obligations retained old values. A mural painter simplified laboring figures into bright curved forms visible across crowded quays. A public reader investigated a councilor’s splendid house and found that its builder also received favorable canal contracts. The reader lost her sponsored platform but continued recitations in market yards. Negotiators began carrying plain cloth to request safe passage, although some courts treated the sign as surrender and others as a request to speak. Confusion allowed creditors to seize people approaching in good faith. Shared standards emerged slowly from recorded incidents rather than a single heroic proclamation.

In the connected record, [The Price of the Same Basket](b012-the-price-of-the-same-basket.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Approach Without Submission](b012-the-approach-without-submission.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how negotiated access and old debts can be revised while households still need the contracts maintaining their canals.

Talassar combines salt marshes, sheltered boat towns, and orchards whose irrigation depends on tidal timing. Merchants trade durable claims alongside perishable cargo, although only an actual witnessed undertaking has force after death. A repaired boat, a borrowed name, and a neglected drain can each determine whether a contract remains practical. Houses that prosper from distant obligations are exposed to local maintenance costs they would prefer to call somebody else’s problem. Seasonal kitchens and landing crews keep accounts that often describe the region more accurately than its ceremonial histories of uninterrupted family success.
