# The Officer’s Surviving Claims

*Dispute · Keth · Historical coverage: 402–1248 AFR*

Descendants seek rights under an imperial office later courts deny. Current households depend on some of its valid undertakings. The hearing separates those finite commitments from the impossible demand to revive an entire vanished jurisdiction.

The dispute contains more than two positions. People with the same formal entitlement may face different practical costs, while opponents can depend upon the same canal, kitchen, or crossing. A complete settlement must describe an executable change rather than award a satisfying phrase to one side. Evidence about a past wrong remains necessary even when compensation cannot restore the original arrangement. Local negotiators separate immediate maintenance from the final assessment of responsibility. This allows a place to remain usable while its inhabitants continue an argument they have good reason not to abandon.

This belongs to The Last Officer and the Eastern Wall. After the shelf empire dissolved in 349, one cistern city retained its officer, school, and provisioning offices. Neighbors called the officer a king, although his own documents refused the title. In 402 a conquering ruler adopted the name of a legendary ancient sovereign and took the city, promising a restored golden age. Later wars involved southern powers and outside specialists whose aid changed local dependency. An occupation divided the city behind a wall, making its eastern rooms a capital others refused to recognize. Weaving schools carried lessons across successive regimes. Their history preserves practical continuity without accepting every conqueror’s claim that an old name made the present order a reborn original.

In the connected record, [Syren the Officer Called King](b036-syren-the-officer-called-king.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Successor’s Explicit Scope](b036-the-successor-s-explicit-scope.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether reunited city households can share cistern access while untangling obligations authenticated by offices whose legitimacy each successor disputed.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
