# Iren of the Exiled Measures

*Person · Suth · Historical coverage: 943–1248 AFR*

Iren carried songs between valleys while recovering from an injured hand. Younger musicians adapted her parts rather than replacing her. Their ensemble’s continuity depended on accommodation, contradicting patrons’ image of rebellion as effortless youthful vigor.

Later accounts make Iren of the Exiled Measures the explanation for an entire local change. The surviving record instead places this life among dependencies: an introduction supplied by another household, materials purchased on credit, and testimony given by people with reasons of their own. A biography that removes those dependencies becomes a useful legend and a poor guide to what happened. Descendants inherit evidence and expectations, rather than the ability to repeat an ancestor’s achievement. The practical question is which part of the achievement can be maintained by ordinary people after the celebrated individual is gone.

This belongs to The Judges Who Walk Between Gardens. Suth’s garden communes elected judges rather than inheriting a sovereign. In 943 a physician reformer sought to reduce the separate courts maintained by armed households and funerary custodians. His ally withdrew support when those institutions threatened revolt. Traveling musicians carried arguments between valleys, blending older measures with instruments learned in exile. An outside authority later occupied the eastern gardens while the western communes retained a different charter. When a public vote ended occupation, departing forces destroyed channels and records. Returning households therefore faced reconstruction before constitutional celebration. The elected judges now walk between settlements to hear claims, because neither the old exemptions nor the new vote can make water flow through a broken canal.

In the connected record, [The West Charter Orchard](b020-the-west-charter-orchard.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Coerced Transfer Review](b020-the-coerced-transfer-review.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how elected courts can rebuild channels while deciding which occupation-era transactions were coerced and which present households depend upon them.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
