# The Rescue Landing

*Event · Avel · Historical coverage: 809–1248 AFR*

The captain arrived with exhausted passengers and insufficient seed. Island households supplied food through finite loans. Later independence speeches call the shore empty, obscuring the very agreements that allowed the rescued people to remain alive.

The event survives through records made for different purposes. A household remembers hunger; a court records jurisdiction; a custodian records the interruption of an observance. Their dates can be reconciled without making their explanations identical. Later commemorations often select a decisive moment because a ceremony needs a beginning and an end. The consequences were less orderly, traveling through marriages, prices, and people who moved away. An honest account follows those consequences far enough to distinguish a temporary outcome from an agreement that still constrains the present.

This belongs to The Rock of Borrowed Refuge. The hollow rock guarding Avel’s southern strait accumulated shrines, walls, and competing harbor names over centuries. In 809 a privateer captain transported people freed from distant labor claims to a settlement beside it. The settlers proclaimed independence but restricted full membership to households arriving through the captain’s patrons. Existing island residents supplied food and knowledge while remaining outside the new council. A performer played two opposed brothers in a harbor drama, giving their rival fears equal weight. The captain’s apprentice mapped shelters omitted from official charts. His work revealed both genuine rescue and new hierarchy, leaving present residents to negotiate freedom without erasing the people already living beneath the celebrated rock.

In the connected record, [The Captain’s Sounding Weight](b017-the-captain-s-sounding-weight.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Equal Landing Witness](b017-the-equal-landing-witness.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether founding passengers and earlier island households can share harbor authority without losing the protective landing agreements that enabled refuge.

Avel’s islands preserve competing versions of the basin’s earliest treaties. Sea crossings depend on sails, weather, and provisions, giving a copied text little authority unless somebody can carry its terms home and explain them. Fishing households share beaches with custodians of drowned foundations. Low water reveals masonry that changes both navigation and historical argument. An island’s isolation can shelter dissent and conceal abuse; its effects depend upon who controls the boats. Agreements are usually bounded by named landing places, because a promise to protect an entire sea would have no workable observance.
