# The Judges’ Graft Road

*Route · Suth · Historical coverage: 943–1248 AFR*

The road follows settlements needing seasonal graft inspections, allowing hearings and practical instruction on one journey. Wealthier communes seek priority visits. Judges publish travel costs and unmet requests so exclusion cannot disappear behind their title of universal service.

A route is an agreement between people maintaining its difficult portions, as much as a line across terrain. Tolls buy repairs only when their recipients are accountable for the work. A passage described as open may still be unusable for children, loaded animals, or travelers without an accepted witness. Seasonal detours preserve movement while distributing its costs to other households. Pilots and guides keep alternative accounts of distance because time, provisions, and exposure matter more than ceremonial measures. Closing one stretch can therefore revise obligations far beyond its immediate neighborhood.

This belongs to The Judges Who Walk Between Gardens. Suth’s garden communes elected judges rather than inheriting a sovereign. In 943 a physician reformer sought to reduce the separate courts maintained by armed households and funerary custodians. His ally withdrew support when those institutions threatened revolt. Traveling musicians carried arguments between valleys, blending older measures with instruments learned in exile. An outside authority later occupied the eastern gardens while the western communes retained a different charter. When a public vote ended occupation, departing forces destroyed channels and records. Returning households therefore faced reconstruction before constitutional celebration. The elected judges now walk between settlements to hear claims, because neither the old exemptions nor the new vote can make water flow through a broken canal.

In the connected record, [The Coerced Transfer Review](b020-the-coerced-transfer-review.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Coerced Transfer Review](b020-the-coerced-transfer-review.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how elected courts can rebuild channels while deciding which occupation-era transactions were coerced and which present households depend upon them.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
