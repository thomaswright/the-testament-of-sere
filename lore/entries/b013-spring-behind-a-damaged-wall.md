# Spring Behind a Damaged Wall

*Text · Suth · Historical coverage: 1059–1248 AFR*

The play’s narrator admits desires she cannot act upon without harming people she loves. Patrons seek a decisive moral ending. Performers preserve the hesitant conclusion because repairing a household may require commitments that remain emotionally unsettled.

Copies disagree in ways that matter. A shortened phrase can remove a condition, and a copied date can imply that a later amendment belonged to the original undertaking. Dry writing preserves evidence but cannot make an oath binding by itself. Readers must distinguish a record of consent from a writer’s assertion that consent occurred. Marginal notes are sometimes the only surviving testimony of people excluded from the formal version. Establishing a trustworthy reading therefore depends on comparing custody, material, and ordinary expenses, as well as elegant arguments about wording.

This belongs to Spring in the Salt House. A small garden town prospered by exporting preserving salts while depending on imported grain. Its merchants claimed descent from a legendary artisan capital, using that story to justify exclusive membership in the exchange court. In 1059 a returning traveler stayed with a married friend whose garden had deteriorated after injury. A quiet play about the household’s competing loyalties became unexpectedly popular. An economic reader used its ordinary transactions to show how trusted institutions reduced bargaining costs for members while burdening outsiders. The salt slump exposed those outsiders’ vulnerability. Excavated workshop remains then complicated the claimed artisan lineage, leaving the town to defend useful cooperation without the exclusive origin story that had supported its authority.

In the connected record, [The Salt Garden Burrowing Dove](b013-the-salt-garden-burrowing-dove.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Contribution Admission Test](b013-the-contribution-admission-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the exchange court can admit workers lacking prestigious ancestry while protecting the shared guarantees that make ordinary trade possible.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
