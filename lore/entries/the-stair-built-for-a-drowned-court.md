# The Stair Built for a Drowned Court

*Underwater ruin · Keth · Historical coverage: Before 0–1248 AFR*

Liwen saw only six steps. Those who publish pictures of a complete drowned palace have invented the remainder. The visible stair descends from a platform beneath the deep cistern, turns behind a square support, and continues beyond the range of light brought under his inverted bell. Its upper tread bears a groove made by something repeatedly dragged across it. The groove does not establish a throne, a coffin, or a particular ruler's passage.

Small pale fish rested above the lower tread during his first inspection. When the rope crew lifted the bell, they followed its shadow and dispersed at the cistern's shallower wall. This ordinary behavior appears in the crew's account beside an extraordinary observation: Liwen's lamp reflection descended one more step after he had stopped moving. He turned the lamp away and the reflection vanished. Neither he nor the surface watchers saw a person beneath him. A subsequent trial with an empty lamp cage produced nothing comparable.

The harbor successor court seized upon the stair as the entrance to its lost ancestral seat. Its oldest shore recitation mentions a square support and six obligations, a resemblance interesting enough to investigate and insufficient to establish title. Salt readers have found deposits at the recovered edge unlike those on the present cistern roof. The difference could indicate an older water regime or stone reused from another place. The Painted Quarter's layered foundations have already shown how persuasive an apparent age can become when later builders carry old material into a new arrangement.

A third inspection was proposed in 1230 and canceled after the rope passed through a cracked guide ring. The apprentice responsible for checking the ring received public blame. The crew ledger showed that a patron had declined its replacement because the ceremonial bell already exceeded the budget. Recording that refusal has done more to preserve later divers than any invocation of the supposed court below. The broken ring hangs in the preparation shelter, wrapped so its edge cannot cut a visitor.

In 1248 the upper stair remains submerged. Custodians permit diagrams of the inspected portion and mark the rest with open space. Their restraint has become unfashionable: collectors prefer a complete palace, and some interpreters sell an account of six drowned rulers who await the right descendant. Liwen has not endorsed it. He describes the cold, the limited air, the fish, the moving reflection, and the moment he asked to be raised. The ruin's unresolved extent is enough to trouble sleep. Nobody needs to furnish it with a finished dynasty.
