# The Artisan Origin Stamp

*Artifact · Suth · Historical coverage: 1059–1248 AFR*

A prestigious stamp proves long use within the court but no descent from its supposed capital. Its authenticity therefore supports an institutional history different from its legend. Members must decide whether practical continuity can sustain their authority without the older claim.

Material inspection places limits on the object’s legend. Repairs, wear, and changes in custody can establish that it was used repeatedly without proving every story attached to it. Its value is relational: somebody can authenticate it, somebody needs it for an observance, and somebody may suffer if it is removed. An expensive replacement would not automatically inherit those relations. Custodians therefore record loans and repairs as carefully as ceremonial appearances. A collector who purchases the object but ignores its attached undertaking may own something that cannot safely be taken home.

This belongs to Spring in the Salt House. A small garden town prospered by exporting preserving salts while depending on imported grain. Its merchants claimed descent from a legendary artisan capital, using that story to justify exclusive membership in the exchange court. In 1059 a returning traveler stayed with a married friend whose garden had deteriorated after injury. A quiet play about the household’s competing loyalties became unexpectedly popular. An economic reader used its ordinary transactions to show how trusted institutions reduced bargaining costs for members while burdening outsiders. The salt slump exposed those outsiders’ vulnerability. Excavated workshop remains then complicated the claimed artisan lineage, leaving the town to defend useful cooperation without the exclusive origin story that had supported its authority.

In the connected record, [The Witness’s Seed Portion](b013-the-witness-s-seed-portion.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Contribution Admission Test](b013-the-contribution-admission-test.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether the exchange court can admit workers lacking prestigious ancestry while protecting the shared guarantees that make ordinary trade possible.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
