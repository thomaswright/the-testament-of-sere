# Terrace Wall Grafting

*Trade · Suth · Historical coverage: 982–1248 AFR*

Gardeners plant selected roots through wall beds to bind soil, requiring careful pruning so roots do not split masonry. Courts classify this skilled work as ceremonial service. Practitioners seek payment schedules and authority over repairs rather than praise for inherited devotion.

The trade depends upon tools, training, reliable materials, and people who perform work its public account barely mentions. A skilled craftsperson can improve a process, yet every batch still requires hands and a season of available materials. Output remains bounded by fuel, animals, weather, and the hands available to repair equipment. Prices carry the history of these constraints into households far from the workshop. A commercial undertaking that survives its maker can preserve useful cooperation or prevent successors from adapting to changed conditions. The present negotiation concerns how to retain knowledge while releasing an obligation nobody can now perform.

This belongs to The Steps That Carry a Song. Suth’s burial terraces expanded for centuries, accommodating rulers, gardeners, pilgrims, and people denied formal household graves. In 982 a valley campaign classified several gardening communities as supporters of an armed revolt and destroyed their water records. Survivors carried names through sung measures adapted from an older harvesting repertoire. Runners relayed the verses across terraces, sometimes abandoning a timed contest to support an exhausted companion. Later courts trusted official burial inscriptions more than the songs. Comparing the two revealed that prestigious monuments had reused stone from destroyed communal channels. The present dispute concerns testimony embedded in performance and material, neither of which supplies an easy complete account.

In the connected record, [The Monuments’ Maintenance Claim](b005-the-monuments-maintenance-claim.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Testimony in Measure Rule](b005-the-testimony-in-measure-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: who must maintain the terraces while descendants seek acknowledgment of the gardens their monuments displaced.

The Suth valleys cultivate grafted orchards and irrigated gardens amid dry hills. Food production depends on precise local knowledge: when to open a channel, which rootstock tolerates mineral water, and how many hands a harvest requires. Asylum customs let strangers work under temporary names, but temporary recognition does not automatically convey inheritance. The same gardens can preserve an old promise and expose its injustice. A court that assigns water without accounting for the labor of keeping it clean may issue a valid judgment that no household can afford to obey.
