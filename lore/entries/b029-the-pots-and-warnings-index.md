# The Pots and Warnings Index

*Text · Orun · Historical coverage: 873–1248 AFR*

An index follows messages through shared household utensils and food deliveries. It supplements military records rather than replacing them. Its small transactions reveal how information moved through people whose names official command histories rarely preserve.

Copies disagree in ways that matter. A shortened phrase can remove a condition, and a copied date can imply that a later amendment belonged to the original undertaking. Dry writing preserves evidence but cannot make an oath binding by itself. Readers must distinguish a record of consent from a writer’s assertion that consent occurred. Marginal notes are sometimes the only surviving testimony of people excluded from the formal version. Establishing a trustworthy reading therefore depends on comparing custody, material, and ordinary expenses, as well as elegant arguments about wording.

This belongs to The Diary of the Fog Muster. A provincial commander pursued raiders into a narrow lakeside road during morning mist, convinced that delay would damage his reputation. His force was trapped between water and hidden slopes. Survivors returned to towns where a separate levy attempt had triggered running confrontations along several roads. A household diarist recorded provisions, embarrassment, conflicting rumors, and the gap between expected courage and actual fear. A comic performer later adapted the diary’s domestic quarrels. Authorities preferred a grand account of one awakening people, but the small records reveal uneven preparation and changing choices. They also preserve meals, messages, and repairs that enabled resistance more reliably than the flattering language of destiny.

In the connected record, [The Mist That Holds Footsteps](b029-the-mist-that-holds-footsteps.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Levy’s Stated Purpose](b029-the-levy-s-stated-purpose.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: how commemorations can honor real resistance without using its heroic image to impose another compulsory levy on the households that sustained it.

The Orun channels shift across a landscape whose legal descriptions often name vanished banks. Fishers, reed workers, and gardeners share access without sharing a single account of ownership. Transport depends on shallow boats and pilots who read changes in sediment. Salt reaches some plots well before neighboring plots, making an apparently equal assessment unfair in practice. An ancestral claim can survive a change in the channel, but its observance may become impossible at the old site. Communities must decide whether to move the observance, compensate its claimants, or leave the affected water unused.
