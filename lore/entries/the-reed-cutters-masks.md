# The Reed Cutters' Masks

*Custom · Orun · Historical coverage: 782–1248 AFR*

Reed cutters cover their faces during the annual Heret maintenance assembly, though nobody agrees that they did so during the ambush itself. The oldest account mentions cloth against insects and smoke. A victory poem written forty years later describes faceless warriors emerging from the marsh. The counting houses subsequently used that poem to argue that the attackers could not be recognized as lawful witnesses. The masks began as protection, became a heroic image, and then became evidence against the people who wore them.

The present custom reverses the accusation. Each masked participant places a named tally beside the maintenance basin before speaking. The visible face is withheld, but responsibility is explicitly recorded. A debt holder cannot identify a speaker by sight and intimidate their relatives during the meeting; the assembly can still hold the speaker accountable afterward through the tally. This arrangement is imperfect because powerful families know their debtors' voices. Children therefore read some statements for adults, flattening local accents through a deliberately monotonous recital.

Masks are made from woven reed, cloth, or thin bark. Expensive carved examples are frowned upon because they advertise the wearer's household. A speaker who insists upon ornaments must place them on the common table until the assembly ends. One counting-house representative repeatedly brings a gold-edged mask, pays the resulting fine, and treats the incident as proof of generosity. Cutters have begun buying grain with his fines before he arrives, reducing his yearly performance to an accounting convenience.

Outsiders often interpret the ceremony as worship of a hidden marsh god. There is no such god in the surviving undertakings. The assembly negotiates sluice repairs, working hours, and injury compensation. Its anonymity serves living people facing recognizable pressures. In 1248, the participants are debating whether newcomers without registered tallies should be allowed to speak through a sponsor. The choice will determine whether the custom continues protecting vulnerable workers or merely conceals the authority of an established group behind a memorable local image.
