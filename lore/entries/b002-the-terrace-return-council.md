# The Terrace Return Council

*Institution · Keth · Historical coverage: 846–1248 AFR*

The council combines descendants of excluded households with current cistern workers. Some seek land restitution; others need uninterrupted water. Its meetings separate urgent repairs from claims of ownership, borrowing the kitchen witnesses’ method of recording actual provision before announcing benevolence.

The institution’s public account emphasizes its founding purpose, while daily accounts reveal exceptions, unpaid work, and compromises with neighbors. Membership does not imply agreement. A clerk, a patron, and a laborer can defend the same arrangement for incompatible reasons. Its authority is strongest where it provides something people actually need and weakest where it claims that need as proof of permanent jurisdiction. Reform requires deciding who will perform the useful work afterward. Simply abolishing the name may leave the obligation in the hands of less accountable successors.

This belongs to The Crown of Peeled Stone. In 846, Commander Darr declared himself emperor of the chalk shelves and borrowed heavily to furnish a coronation cistern. His experimenter discovered that repeatedly peeling a dark stone produced sheets sensitive to wet fingerprints. Darr used the sheets to stage an assembly approving his annexation of neighboring terraces. Armed attendants controlled admission, and absent households were counted as consenting. After his fall in 871, the court identified a picturesque fortress as the rebels’ last refuge. Workmen later found the real shelter beneath a humble plaster yard, where fingerprints preserved evidence of people excluded from the imperial vote. The discovery endangered both victorious families and the fallen ruler’s heirs.

In the connected record, [The Second Coronation Funeral](b002-the-second-coronation-funeral.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Unpressed Consent Rule](b002-the-unpressed-consent-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether authenticated exclusion can revise ownership without depriving present residents of the cisterns they maintain.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
