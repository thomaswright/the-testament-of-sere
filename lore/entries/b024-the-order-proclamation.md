# The Order Proclamation

*Event · Vey · Historical coverage: 1138–1248 AFR*

The administration promised predictable courts after chaotic disputes. It also removed neighborhood assemblies from decisions. Household accounts record both improved delivery schedules and lost opportunities to challenge assessments, resisting a simple verdict that stability meant either universal rescue or universal ruin.

The event survives through records made for different purposes. A household remembers hunger; a court records jurisdiction; a custodian records the interruption of an observance. Their dates can be reconciled without making their explanations identical. Later commemorations often select a decisive moment because a ceremony needs a beginning and an end. The consequences were less orderly, traveling through marriages, prices, and people who moved away. An honest account follows those consequences far enough to distinguish a temporary outcome from an agreement that still constrains the present.

This belongs to The Mothers of the Painted Quarter. Vey’s painted quarter grew around an island crossing, accumulating shrines, schools, crowded rooms, and repeated civic occupations. In 1138 a new administration promised stability after turmoil and organized trades into supervised corporations. A dramatist filled the quarter with bright household stories about mothers, chosen kin, concealed desire, and irreverent survival. Officials tolerated festive color while excluding mass participation from decisions. A historical reader exposed a regime chronicler’s forged letters, omitted conditions, and selective quotation. Theater households supplied records the chronicler dismissed as domestic trivia. Their alliance did not make every play factual; it established that an orderly public narrative could be contradicted by precisely documented lives outside its preferred institutions.

In the connected record, [The Chronicler’s False Letter](b024-the-chronicler-s-false-letter.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Evidence in Full Rule](b024-the-evidence-in-full-rule.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether civic stability can survive a public account acknowledging the quarter’s excluded households rather than requiring their histories to remain picturesque entertainment.

Vey households measure wealth partly in dependable access to a public landing. Their markets exchange grain, lamp oil, glazed jars, and legal testimony. A witness can authenticate a transaction but cannot provide the labor needed to fulfill it. Seasonal crews clean the same embankments on which courts announce judgments, and a decision that ignores those crews soon meets an expensive physical limit. Flood deposits preserve small objects while destroying the arrangement that once made their meaning obvious. Recovering an object is therefore easier than establishing what anybody was entitled to do with it.
