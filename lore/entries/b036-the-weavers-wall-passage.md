# The Weavers’ Wall Passage

*Route · Keth · Historical coverage: 402–1248 AFR*

Weaving teachers crossed through a narrow work permit route carrying samples and lesson leaves. Guards disputed their authority but needed textiles. The passage preserved knowledge through constrained cooperation rather than an uninterrupted freedom later commemorations sometimes imagine.

A route is an agreement between people maintaining its difficult portions, as much as a line across terrain. Tolls buy repairs only when their recipients are accountable for the work. A passage described as open may still be unusable for children, loaded animals, or travelers without an accepted witness. Seasonal detours preserve movement while distributing its costs to other households. Pilots and guides keep alternative accounts of distance because time, provisions, and exposure matter more than ceremonial measures. Closing one stretch can therefore revise obligations far beyond its immediate neighborhood.

This belongs to The Last Officer and the Eastern Wall. After the shelf empire dissolved in 349, one cistern city retained its officer, school, and provisioning offices. Neighbors called the officer a king, although his own documents refused the title. In 402 a conquering ruler adopted the name of a legendary ancient sovereign and took the city, promising a restored golden age. Later wars involved southern powers and outside specialists whose aid changed local dependency. An occupation divided the city behind a wall, making its eastern rooms a capital others refused to recognize. Weaving schools carried lessons across successive regimes. Their history preserves practical continuity without accepting every conqueror’s claim that an old name made the present order a reborn original.

In the connected record, [The Successor’s Explicit Scope](b036-the-successor-s-explicit-scope.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Successor’s Explicit Scope](b036-the-successor-s-explicit-scope.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether reunited city households can share cistern access while untangling obligations authenticated by offices whose legitimacy each successor disputed.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
