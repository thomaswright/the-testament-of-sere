# Calendar Conversion Teaching

*Trade · Keth · Historical coverage: 402–1248 AFR*

Teachers compare dated household transactions to reconcile rival calendars. Errors affect inheritance and fees. The craft is slow and ordinary, yet essential to making reunion real for people whose lives crossed regimes with incompatible official measures of time.

The trade depends upon tools, training, reliable materials, and people who perform work its public account barely mentions. A skilled craftsperson can improve a process, yet every batch still requires hands and a season of available materials. Output remains bounded by fuel, animals, weather, and the hands available to repair equipment. Prices carry the history of these constraints into households far from the workshop. A commercial undertaking that survives its maker can preserve useful cooperation or prevent successors from adapting to changed conditions. The present negotiation concerns how to retain knowledge while releasing an obligation nobody can now perform.

This belongs to The Last Officer and the Eastern Wall. After the shelf empire dissolved in 349, one cistern city retained its officer, school, and provisioning offices. Neighbors called the officer a king, although his own documents refused the title. In 402 a conquering ruler adopted the name of a legendary ancient sovereign and took the city, promising a restored golden age. Later wars involved southern powers and outside specialists whose aid changed local dependency. An occupation divided the city behind a wall, making its eastern rooms a capital others refused to recognize. Weaving schools carried lessons across successive regimes. Their history preserves practical continuity without accepting every conqueror’s claim that an old name made the present order a reborn original.

In the connected record, [The Officer’s Surviving Claims](b036-the-officer-s-surviving-claims.md) preserves another part of this history. The terms recorded under [The Successor’s Explicit Scope](b036-the-successor-s-explicit-scope.md) delimit the local obligation; they do not grant a ruler unlimited command over the dead. The unresolved issue in 1248 is concrete: whether reunited city households can share cistern access while untangling obligations authenticated by offices whose legitimacy each successor disputed.

Keth communities inhabit chalk shelves where rainfall must be collected before it drains into inaccessible fissures. Builders maintain cistern roofs, plastered channels, and terraces with tools that families repair for generations. Burial niches compete with storage rooms for dry space. Ordinary seepage can resemble ancestral interference, so reliable testimony begins with inspecting masonry and comparing neighboring water levels. The most powerful household is seldom able to sustain a settlement alone. It depends on masons, charcoal carriers, and shepherds whose labor does not appear in the grand genealogies carved above the cistern doors.
