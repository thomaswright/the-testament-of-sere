# Suth

Suth’s southern inland valleys cultivate grafted orchards and carefully scheduled irrigation. Rootstocks, soil, water minerals, labor, and weather determine what grows. Star diagrams and prestigious cultivar names cannot replace that knowledge. Garden walls carry repairs from successive households, and their beauty often conceals precise practical work.

Asylum customs recognize strangers temporarily without automatically conveying every inherited right. Walking judges reduce travel costs while exposing exclusions within elected self-government. Protected meeting gardens remain inhabited productive places, not empty scenery made neutral by a ruler’s announcement.

The burial terraces preserve rulers, gardeners, lost communities, and stone reused from destroyed channels. Relay songs carry names through a landscape whose official inscriptions omitted them. Elsewhere, handcraft sweet workshops and a boarding school show useful generosity entangled with paternal authority.

In 1248, the Last Season practice offers a narrow substitute where a household’s intended recipient has vanished after careful inquiry. It cannot invent ownership of every unresolved ruin. Begin with the terrace repertoire for memory, the Peace Garden for finite cooperation, or the sweetmaking town for everyday pleasure whose food, fuel, and care remain accountable to real material limits.
